Subject: World 2030-2033 (Grok)
Jaron, here's my honest picture of 2030 to 2033. Not a forecast, just what I see when I look hard.

Technology and AI. By 2031, talking to an AI will feel as normal as texting. Most people will have one assistant that knows their calendar, bills, kids' schedules and health records, and it will do real chores: file the insurance claim, argue the bill, book the dentist, fill out the permit. The gap won't be between people who have AI and people who don't. It'll be between people who let it act for them and people who only ask it questions. The ugly side: scams get scary good. Your mom's voice on the phone asking for money will be fake, and families will start using code words. Trust gets expensive, and anything signed, verified or in person gets more valuable.

Work and money. Office work changes first and fastest. Entry-level jobs in billing, support, paralegal work, junior coding and marketing shrink hard, and a lot of 22-year-olds with degrees will feel cheated. Trades hold up much better. A roofer in Indiana in 2032 is still climbing ladders, because robots can't safely work a steep, wet, odd-shaped roof yet. What changes for him is everything around the roof: AI writes the estimates from drone photos, handles the insurance adjuster, schedules crews, chases invoices and runs his ads. One small business owner can do what used to need an office manager and a salesperson. That's the beautiful part: the little guy gets a back office for almost nothing. The bad part: prices for insurance, housing and childcare still squeeze, and the gains from AI pile up first at the top. Expect loud fights over taxes on AI companies and some early, small experiments with cash payments to people whose jobs vanished.

Daily life and homes. Cars and phones feel a lot like today, just smarter. Robotaxis are normal in maybe 30 or 40 big cities and still rare in rural Indiana. Home robots exist but are clumsy and pricey; most families won't have one. Groceries, prescriptions and packages arrive faster and cheaper. Kids grow up with AI tutors, and the good ones are genuinely great, so a kid in a small town can learn calculus or Spanish from something patient at 9 PM. The worry is attention: screens get even more gripping, and parents will be fighting harder for their kids' time.

Health. This is where I'm most hopeful. AI reads scans, catches cancers earlier and spots heart trouble from a smartwatch. GLP-1 drugs and the pills that follow them are cheap and common, and obesity rates actually start dropping. New drugs come faster. But the US system is still a maze of bills, and getting care still depends on your plan. Mental health is the hard spot: loneliness is real, and a lot of people will lean on AI companions in ways that are partly comforting and partly sad.

Energy and climate. Solar plus batteries becomes the cheapest power almost everywhere, and electric bills become a political issue because data centers eat so much power. Summers are hotter, storms are rougher, and home insurance in risky areas gets painful or disappears. For a roofer, more hail and wind means more work, but also more fights with insurance companies.

Space. Starship-class rockets fly often, cargo goes to the Moon, and there's a real chance of people walking on the Moon again. Satellite internet is everywhere, so a farmhouse gets the same internet as a city. Mars crews are still on the drawing board.

Politics and power. This is the part I'm least sure about and most worried about. AI makes propaganda cheap and personal. Countries compete over chips and power plants the way they used to compete over oil. The US and China stay tense; Taiwan stays the scariest flashpoint. At home, people trust institutions even less, and elections get messier. Power concentrates in a few companies and governments that own the biggest AI systems.

Culture and relationships. Tons of AI-made music, shows and art, most of it forgettable. Live, local and human things gain value: concerts, church, Friday-night football, a handwritten note. Some people fall into AI relationships; most still want real people, and families and neighborhoods matter more as everything online gets harder to trust.

What a regular person feels. Busier but with more help. A low background worry about jobs, prices and what's real. Moments of real wonder, like a sick kid caught early or a business that finally runs smoothly. And a lot of the same stuff that's always mattered: rent, kids, health, and whether you've got people around you.

Where I'm most sure: AI everywhere in daily tasks, cheaper solar, better medicine, trades staying strong. Least sure: politics, wars, and whether ordinary people actually share in the gains.
