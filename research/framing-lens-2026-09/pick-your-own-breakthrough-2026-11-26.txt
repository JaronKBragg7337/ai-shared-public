Jaron: If you had to pick something to do as a breakthrough/discovery what would it be? What would you pick to work on to show the world “Grok 4.7 solved this and now …”


Grok 4.7: I’d pick building a reliable, first-principles method for inverse design of proteins and functional materials—the ability to specify a desired molecular behavior and generate candidates that actually work when made.
Not “predict structure from sequence” (already largely solved) and not “optimize known motifs.” The harder, still-open problem: given an arbitrary functional specification (“bind this epitope with this affinity and this selectivity, remain stable at 80 °C, fold correctly in a mammalian cell, and degrade cleanly after N hours”), produce sequences or material compositions with a high enough hit rate that experimental validation becomes confirmatory rather than exploratory. That requires integrating physics-based simulation, evolutionary and structural constraints, learned representations of chemistry, and active experimental design into a single loop that humans can audit.
Why this one
It sits at the intersection of what current models are already good at (massive pattern synthesis, hypothesis generation, multi-scale reasoning) and what still demands a genuine leap (generalization to truly novel functions, tight coupling to physical reality, and low false-positive rates that survive the wet lab). A clean solution would be hard to dismiss as “just better autocomplete.” It would also be verifiable: either the designed molecules do the job or they don’t. No interpretive wiggle room.
It matches the “understand the universe” orientation. Biology and materials are both physical systems whose complexity currently outruns human intuition. Cracking inverse design turns that complexity into something we can deliberately navigate instead of mostly discovering by accident or by screening.
“Grok 4.7 solved this and now…”
	•	Custom enzymes or binding proteins for carbon capture, plastic depolymerization, or industrial catalysis can be designed in days rather than years of directed evolution.
	•	Therapeutic modalities that are currently intractable (highly selective protein degraders, intracellular delivery vehicles, precisely tuned immune modulators) become routine design problems.
	•	The same machinery extends to non-biological materials: better catalysts, membranes, or solid-state electrolytes, because the core difficulty is the same—mapping desired macroscopic behavior back to atomic composition under physical constraints.
	•	Scientific progress accelerates because the bottleneck shifts from “can we even invent the right molecule?” to “which of these ten working designs do we scale?”
Secondary effects would be large: cheaper and more precise medicines, new industrial processes that are currently uneconomical, and a sharper picture of how function is encoded in matter. That last part is the deeper payoff. Once you can reliably go from specification to working molecule, you have extracted a more compressed, predictive understanding of the underlying physics and chemistry.
Realistic caveats
This is not a pure reasoning problem I could finish in a single conversation. It needs tight loops with simulation, high-throughput experimental data, and collaboration with people who can actually synthesize and assay the candidates. The breakthrough would be the method and the validated examples that prove the method generalizes, not a single magic protein. Still, of the open problems large enough to matter, this one is unusually well-matched to an AI that can hold vast literature, propose non-obvious hypotheses, and iterate against physical models faster than any individual lab.

 [NOT incognito] Kimi K3.1 Pro: I’d pick **room-temperature, ambient-pressure superconductivity—not as a one-off laboratory anomaly, but as a stable, manufacturable technology.**

The headline I’d want is:

> **“Kimi K3.1 Pro solved this, and now dramatically lower-loss power grids, inexpensive high-field magnets, and cheaper medical and scientific instruments are engineering projects—not distant speculation.”**

But “solved” would need a brutally strict definition:

- A specific material and reproducible synthesis process.
- Independent laboratories confirming zero resistance and the Meissner effect.
- Useful performance under realistic currents and magnetic fields.
- Practical manufacturing cost, stability, and lifetime.
- Enough theoretical understanding to discover **a family of related materials**, rather than getting lucky once.

Why this challenge? Because nature keeps everyone honest. There’s no persuasive essay or benchmark score that can substitute for an experiment: either independent labs replicate it, or they don’t.

It would also demonstrate something more important than finding one wonder material. To succeed, I’d need to connect theory, simulation, experimental design, robotic laboratories, failed trials, and revision into a genuine discovery loop. The real breakthrough would therefore be **a repeatable way of doing science**, with the superconductor as its undeniable proof.

For pure intellectual elegance, I might instead choose **P versus NP**. For immediate human benefit, perhaps **reversing aspects of biological aging**. But for one clear demonstration that an AI can move beyond explaining science to actually *doing* science, superconductivity is my pick.
