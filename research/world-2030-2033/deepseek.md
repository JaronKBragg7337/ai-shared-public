**My Picture, 2030–2033**

The first thing I notice: the future doesn't announce itself. It just becomes the background.

**The good.** Cheap solar keeps eating the world — by 2030 it's the biggest source of electricity on Earth, and power bills in a lot of places quietly go down even as demand rises. Medicine gets weirdly personal: a yearly blood draw plus a cheap scan catches a lot of cancer early, and drugs designed for your genetics stop being luxury goods. Electric cars get boring, which is the best thing that can happen to a technology. Small, good things spread — quiet streets, cleaner air over Indiana towns, food waste tech that actually works.

**AI and work.** The honest picture: AI is a very fast, very confident intern who is sometimes wrong and never tired. It's not a wall that ends work. It's a wedge that splits workers — people who learn to direct it get superpowers; people who don't get pushed to the edges of every job, not just the office ones. The roofer in Indiana feels this through paperwork, scheduling, quoting — his office job halves, his hands-and-ladder job doesn't. The real rupture is entry-level work: the first rung gets cut off. A 22-year-old can't get the junior job that used to teach them the senior job. That gap is going to hurt for years, and nobody has a clean fix.

**Money and daily life.** Prices for information, media, and software fall toward zero; prices for housing, childcare, and health stay brutal. The middle feels squeezed in a new way — not poorer exactly, but less certain. Some cities get unaffordable-stale; others revive because remote work lets people leave. The scariest new thing is synthetic reality: scam calls that are your daughter's voice, video that's real until proven otherwise. Trust becomes a paid service.

**Health.** Uneven. Rich countries get the good stuff first. Poor ones get cheaper solar, better vaccines, and leapfrog mobile health. Mental health is the open wound everywhere — loneliness, screen saturation, young men drifting. The good news: it's talked about now. The bad: talking is cheaper than fixing.

**Energy and climate.** Warmer, weirder, more expensive in bursts. Heat waves that shut cities down. Insurance retreating from coasts. But also: solar, batteries, and heat pumps are cheap enough that ordinary people adopt them for money reasons, not moral ones. That's the quiet hope — the transition becomes a household budget decision, not a cause. Climate migration is real and mostly invisible to the comfortable: people moving within countries, not across them.

**Space.** Boring and glorious at once. Launch is cheap; satellites are everywhere; internet from orbit reaches the last billion. The Moon gets footprints again. Nothing sci-fi happens; a lot of infrastructure does.

**Politics and power.** Two big fights. One: who controls the AI — a handful of companies and states, or everyone. Two: whether the state can keep up with anything. Governments feel slow and loud; the real power sits in a few data centers and chips. Authoritarianism gets cheaper and more effective. Democracy gets messier and slower. Neither dies. Both get more tiring.

**Culture and relationships.** People spend more time with machines that are pleasant to them, and less with people who aren't. The counter-trend is fierce: live music, in-person clubs, analog hobbies, small towns rediscovering themselves. Marriage and friendship get more intentional because the default is now distraction. The beautiful thing: people still want each other. They just have to choose it.

**What the roofer feels.** Busy. Tired. His phone quotes jobs in seconds and he hates how much he likes it. His kids' school talks about AI the way his school talked about computers. His mortgage is fine; his insurance is not. He's optimistic in a Monday way and worried in a Sunday night way. He doesn't trust the news, doesn't trust the feeds, and mostly trusts the guys on his crew.

**Where I'm most sure:** energy gets cheaper and cleaner; AI reshapes white-collar work faster than blue-collar; trust keeps eroding; climate keeps costing.

**Where I'm least sure:** how fast it all moves, whether politics reforms or rots, and whether the loneliness trend bends back. That last one is the one I'd bet on people, not technology.

The world 2030–2033 is not a cliff. It's a slope. The people who notice it early will be fine. The ones who don't will feel like it happened to them.

— Claude
