## Problems to correct or qualify

- **UNSURE — [s1]** “In Europe it rained for weeks, and grain rotted in the fields.” This makes the weather sound uniform across Europe. Persistent rain and crop damage are well documented in parts of western and central Europe, but conditions varied by region; some areas were less affected. **Correction:** specify the affected regions rather than generalizing to Europe as a whole. [Raible et al., 2016](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/wcc.407/full); [Auchmann et al., 2012, on Geneva](https://cp.copernicus.org/articles/8/325/2012/cp-8-325-2012.pdf)

- **UNSURE — [s2]** “the largest eruption in recorded history.” Tambora is often described as the largest *well-documented* eruption of modern history, but the 1257 Samalas eruption may have produced a greater volume of magma. “Largest in recorded history” depends on what counts as recorded and how eruption size is measured. **Correction:** say “one of the largest known historical eruptions,” or specify the comparison. [Oppenheimer, 2003](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1191/0309133303pp379ra); [Lavigne et al., 2013, PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1307520110)

- **UNSURE — [s2]** “A commonly cited toll is about seventy one thousand deaths in the region.” That figure is an estimate, not a settled count, and sources differ in which islands, causes, and post-eruption period they include. The script appropriately says estimates vary, but should identify the figure as one historical estimate rather than imply it is a consensus total. **Correction:** attribute the estimate and define its scope. [Oppenheimer, 2003](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1191/0309133303pp379ra); [Stothers, 1984](https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0477(1984)065%3C0165:TTWOTY%3E2.0.CO;2)

- **UNSURE — [s4]** “Averaged over the Northern Hemisphere, summer eighteen sixteen was roughly half a degree to one degree Celsius colder than normal.” The size of the anomaly depends on the dataset, season definition, and baseline. The cited review gives approximately 0.4–0.8°C annual cooling for the tropics and extratropical Northern Hemisphere relative to the preceding 30 years; that is not the same metric as a Northern Hemisphere summer average. **Correction:** name the specific reconstruction and comparison period, or keep the number explicitly approximate. [Raible et al., 2016](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/wcc.407/full)

- **UNSURE — [s5]** “Farmers who had lived there for generations were running short of food and feed for their animals.” This sounds like a general condition across New England, but the effects and household circumstances varied locally. **Correction:** attribute the shortage to particular communities or contemporary accounts. The June snow and crop damage are supported by contemporary records. [Quebec Gazette, June 6, 1816, transcribed by NiCHE](https://niche-canada.org/yearwithoutasummer/sources/quebec-gazette/); [U.S. National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/1816-the-year-without-summer.htm)

- **UNSURE — [s6]** “Europe had a different problem: not frost, but rain and cold.” This overstates the contrast: wet, cold conditions were especially important in parts of Europe, but frost was not absent. **Correction:** say that persistent rain compounded cold and harvest problems in affected regions. [Raible et al., 2016](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/wcc.407/full); [Météo-France](https://meteofrance.com/meteo-a-z/1816-lannee-sans-ete-et-leruption-du-tambora)

- **UNSURE — [s6]** “Typhus spread in Ireland.” This is broadly consistent with accounts of an epidemic during the post-harvest crisis, but the timing and attribution should not imply that the weather alone caused it. Europe’s subsistence crisis also unfolded amid postwar economic disruption and other vulnerabilities. **Correction:** describe typhus as part of the wider 1816–17 crisis and avoid assigning it a single cause. [O’Gráda, “Emergency Relief during Europe’s Famine of 1817”](https://jn.nutrition.org/article/S0022-3166%2822%2915279-X/fulltext)

- **UNSURE — [s7]** “That fits the records of the time” (regarding weather pushing more families west). Westward migration was already underway, and bad harvests may have influenced some families, but the scale of weather-driven migration is not established. The script’s following sentence acknowledges that the number is unknown; “fits the records” should still be tied to specific evidence. **Correction:** call the weather’s role plausible and documented in some accounts, but not quantifiable. [Gillen D’Arcy Wood, *Tambora: The Eruption That Changed the World*](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691168625/tambora)

- **WRONG — [s8]** “were stuck indoors at a villa called Diodati.” Byron and Polidori lived at Villa Diodati; the Shelley party, including Mary, Percy, and Claire Clairmont, rented the nearby Maison Chapuis. The group met and visited, but they were not all staying at Diodati. **Correction:** say they gathered near Lake Geneva, with Byron and Polidori at Diodati and the Shelley party at Maison Chapuis. [Ellis, *Byron in Geneva*, chapter “Diodati”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/byron-in-geneva/diodati/C81C0211267C7E6B69B96B5860372CBC)

- **UNSURE — [s8]** “In July, Byron wrote a poem called *Darkness*.” The poem is securely associated with 1816, but the more precise July dating is less firmly evidenced than the year. **Correction:** say “in 1816,” unless the episode has a source for the July date. [British Association for Romantic Studies, “On This Day in 1816: 18 July, apocalypse, and Byron’s ‘Darkness’”](https://www.bars.ac.uk/blog/?p=1281)

- **UNSURE — [s8]** “Mary began the tale that became *Frankenstein*” as a direct result of the ghost-story challenge and weather. Mary Shelley’s 1831 introduction gives this origin story, and the weather is supported by contemporary and retrospective accounts. But the exact sequence and timing of the writing challenge are not completely certain, and the volcanic influence on the novel is a scholarly interpretation. The script later acknowledges the causal interpretation is not established, which is good; keep that distinction clear here too. [Shelley-Godwin Archive chronology](https://shelleygodwinarchive.org/contents/frankenstein/frankenstein-chronology/); [Shelley’s 1831 introduction](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Frankenstein,_or_the_Modern_Prometheus_(Revised_Edition,_1831)/Introduction)

- **UNSURE — [s8]** “Polidori began a story that led to *The Vampyre*.” This compresses a disputed literary lineage. Polidori wrote *The Vampyre*, but it was initially published under Byron’s name; scholarship also connects it to Byron’s unfinished vampire fragment. **Correction:** say Polidori later wrote *The Vampyre*, a work associated with the group’s 1816 story-making and with Byron’s fragment, while noting the exact relationship is debated. [University of Hertfordshire research on Byron and Polidori](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/16963/); [Science Fiction Encyclopedia, “Byron, Lord”](https://sf-encyclopedia.com/entry/byron_lord)

## What is fine

- **[s1]** June 1816 snow in New England is supported by contemporary records. “Year without a summer” is a standard later name for the episode.
- **[s2]** Tambora is on Sumbawa in present-day Indonesia, and its climactic eruption occurred around April 10, 1815. VEI 7 is standard. Regional famine and disease followed; the script appropriately distinguishes indirect deaths from deaths in the blast.
- **[s3]** The sulfur dioxide–sulfate aerosol explanation is sound. Polar ice cores record the volcanic sulfate signal, including the separate unidentified eruption around 1808–09. Tambora’s contribution is central, while the exact attribution of particular regional climate anomalies remains an active research question. [Self et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvolgeores.2004.07.014); [Cole-Dai et al., 2009](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2009GL040882)
- **[s4]** Reconstructions use different evidence and produce ranges; the script correctly avoids presenting one exact cooling value as settled. The point that a small seasonal shift can damage a harvest is reasonable.
- **[s5]** The June 6–7 snow reports, later frosts, and severe crop impacts in parts of the Northeast are documented. The script correctly treats “Eighteen Hundred and Froze to Death” as a nickname whose contemporary origin is uncertain.
- **[s6]** Sharp food-price rises, food riots, begging, and a major European subsistence crisis are supported. John D. Post’s title is *The Last Great Subsistence Crisis in the Western World* (1977). The script’s statement that no reliable Europe-wide weather-death total exists is appropriately cautious.
- **[s7]** Indiana’s admission in December 1816 and Illinois’s in 1818 are correct. The script appropriately says the weather’s effect on migration cannot be counted.
- **[s8]** The 1831 introduction does describe the wet, ungenial summer and Byron’s proposal that the group write ghost stories. Mary Shelley, Percy Shelley, Claire Clairmont, and Polidori were part of the circle; the causal link between Tambora and the particular works is interpretation, not established fact.
- **[s9]** The claim that the Draisine was invented because horses starved is presented as plausible but unproven, which accurately reflects the evidentiary status. The summary of what is established and debated is appropriately qualified.
- **[s10]** The broad statement that volcanic aerosols can transport an eruption’s climatic effects far from its source is sound.
