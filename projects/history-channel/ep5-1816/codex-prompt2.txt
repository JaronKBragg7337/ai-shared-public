[s1] In June of eighteen sixteen, snow fell in New England. In much of western and central Europe it rained for weeks, and harvests failed. People called it the year without a summer. A volcano on the other side of the world is the main suspect. This is History, episode five. What do we actually know, and what is still argued about?
[s2] April, eighteen fifteen. Mount Tambora, on the island of Sumbawa in what is now Indonesia, erupts. The main blast came around April tenth. It is rated seven on the volcanic explosivity scale, one of the largest eruptions in recorded history, and the sound was heard about two thousand kilometers away. Ash, famine and disease followed on the islands nearby. One well-known estimate, by the volcanologist Clive Oppenheimer, is about seventy one thousand deaths on the islands nearby. Other estimates differ, and most of those people died of hunger and disease, not the blast itself.
[s3] Why would an eruption cool the whole planet? The key is not ash, which falls out within weeks. It is sulfur dioxide. Lofted high into the stratosphere, it turns into tiny droplets of sulfuric acid that spread around the world and reflect some sunlight back to space. Ice cores from Greenland and Antarctica hold a large sulfate spike from these years. There is a complication. Another big eruption, never identified, happened around eighteen oh nine, and the sun was also quiet in this period. So scientists still debate how much of the cold to blame on Tambora alone.
[s4] How much cooler did it get? Reconstructions from tree rings, diaries and early thermometers give a range, not one number. One review of reconstructions finds the average cooling over the Northern Hemisphere, in the year after the eruption, was roughly four tenths to eight tenths of a degree Celsius. Some regional summers, in parts of Europe and North America, were colder than that. A degree sounds small. But a late frost or a cold wet summer, in a place where people live on one harvest, can be enough to break a crop.
[s5] New England. Newspapers and diaries describe a cold May. Then, on June sixth and seventh, snow fell in parts of New England, upstate New York and Quebec. Frost returned in July, and again in August. Corn, the staple crop, failed in many places. Accounts from some communities describe shortages of food and of feed for animals. The nickname Eighteen Hundred and Froze to Death is popular, but it is not known to be from that year.
[s6] Europe had a related problem: persistent rain on top of the cold. Records in France, Switzerland, Germany and the British Isles describe a sodden summer, a late harvest, and a poor one. Grain prices climbed sharply, in some places by double. Hungry crowds rioted over bread, and many people left home to beg. Historian John Post called it the last great subsistence crisis in the Western world. An epidemic of typhus struck Ireland during the wider crisis, which also had other causes, such as the aftermath of war. How many died in Europe because of the weather? No reliable total exists, and published figures differ widely.
[s7] And in America, people moved. Farmers in northern New England had already been heading west toward Ohio and beyond, along with others from many states. The cold years of eighteen sixteen and seventeen are often said to have pushed more families to go. That is plausible, and some accounts say so, but how many moved because of the weather, and how many would have moved anyway, is not known. Indiana became a state in December of eighteen sixteen. Illinois followed two years later.
[s8] Now to the story everyone remembers. In June eighteen sixteen, near Lake Geneva in Switzerland, the poets Lord Byron and Percy Shelley, with Mary Shelley, her stepsister Claire Clairmont and Byron's doctor, John Polidori, were together that summer. Byron and Polidori were staying at the Villa Diodati, and the Shelley party at a house nearby, and rain kept them indoors. Mary Shelley later wrote of a wet, ungenial summer with incessant rain. Byron proposed that each of them write a ghost story. By her own later account, Mary began the story that became Frankenstein. Polidori later wrote The Vampyre, a work linked to that summer and to a fragment by Byron. The exact link is debated. That year, Byron also wrote a poem called Darkness, about the sun going out. The weather there is documented. That the volcano shaped these particular works is an attractive idea, but it is an interpretation. They might well have written them in a dry summer, too.
[s9] Some popular claims are more doubtful. One says the first bicycle, Karl Drais's running machine of eighteen seventeen, was invented because horses starved without oats. It is a plausible idea, and some historians repeat it, but it is not firmly proven. So, what is established? Tambora erupted in eighteen fifteen. The summer of eighteen sixteen was unusually cold in the Northern Hemisphere. Harvests failed in parts of Europe and North America. What is debated? The exact size of the cooling, how much is due to Tambora, how many people died, and how much the weather changed the course of events.
