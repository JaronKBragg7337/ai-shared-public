SCRIPT (caption text):
s1: Monday afternoon in America, Tuesday before dawn in Japan, an H3 rocket is set to leave Tanegashima with a spacecraft called MMX on top. Its job: fly to Mars, orbit its little moon Phobos, land on it, scoop up at least ten grams, and bring them home in 2031. This is Space Now: what is launching, what is up there, what is known, and what is still open.

s2: The time, from JAXA's own launch plan: October 20, 2026, at 4:41 and 3 seconds in the morning, Japan time. That is October 19 at 3:41 in the afternoon, US Eastern. If anything slips, backup days run from October 21 to November 7, each with its own time. The rocket is H3 flight ten, in its heaviest form: two main engines, four solid boosters, the long fairing. JAXA streams it live in English.

s3: Here is the first hour and a bit, as published. Boosters drop at about two minutes. The fairing opens at three minutes seventeen. The first stage separates just before five minutes. The second stage burns, coasts for most of an hour, burns again, and at one hour, eleven minutes and twelve seconds it lets MMX go, already on its way to Mars at about eleven kilometres a second. Then a year of quiet.

s4: The spacecraft weighs 4,480 kilograms at launch, most of it fuel, and it is built as three modules. A propulsion module does the pushing to Mars and is dropped there. An exploration module carries the landing legs, the samplers and the science instruments. A return module carries the solar wings, the main antenna, and the capsule that comes back. JAXA leads, with NASA, the French and German space agencies, and ESA.

s5: Why Phobos? It is a lump about 27 kilometres long, dark as coal, circling Mars only 6,000 kilometres up, once every seven hours and thirty nine minutes. Nobody knows where it came from. One idea: an asteroid that Mars captured; it looks like one. The other: debris thrown up by a giant impact on Mars, which would explain its tidy, circular orbit over the equator. Decades of pictures have not settled it.

s6: A sample can. In a lab on Earth, ten grams is a lot: minerals, water locked in them, isotope ratios of hydrogen and oxygen, and a radiometric age. A captured asteroid should still hold water-bearing minerals and carbon. Impact debris would be a cooked mix of Mars and the thing that hit it, drier, with Mars' own isotope fingerprint. JAXA's science team says that is the test: lots of Martian material plus missing volatiles points to impact.

s7: There is a bonus either way. Big impacts on Mars have been spraying rock into space for billions of years, and some of it lands on Phobos. A 2019 model led by Ryuki Hyodo put Martian material in the top layer at a few hundred parts per million. In ten grams, that could be a few dozen grains of Mars, from many ages. That is a model estimate, not a measurement. If it holds, these would be the first pieces of Mars brought back on purpose.

s8: The plan, year by year. Late 2027: arrive at Mars and settle into a path that shadows Phobos, a quasi-satellite orbit; MMX would be the first spacecraft to do that. Then months of mapping: shape, gravity, dozens of candidate landing spots. 2029: drop the rover. The landing and sampling: ESA's page says 2030, the German agency's says 2029. Both say the capsule reaches Australia in 2031. Published plan, not a promise.

s9: The rover is called Idefix, built by France and Germany. It weighs 25 kilograms on Earth. On Phobos, where gravity is about one two-thousandth of ours, it weighs about as much as a few coins. MMX lets it fall from somewhere between 40 and 100 metres. It tumbles, stops, unfolds four wheels and stands itself up, all on its own. Then about 100 days of cameras, a Raman spectrometer and a radiometer, testing the ground where the big ship will land.

s10: The landing itself is the hard part. Radio takes up to 44 minutes there and back, so the ship lands by itself on four legs. Two samplers. A corer on a robot arm pushes more than two centimetres into the ground and holds the core with a shape-memory alloy. A pneumatic sampler, provided by NASA, sits by a footpad and uses a puff of nitrogen to blow loose surface grains into a container. Two kinds of dirt: below the surface and on top of it. JAXA allows for one landing, or two.

s11: Along the way the ship measures everything it can: a telescope camera, a wide colour camera, a laser altimeter, an infrared spectrometer from France for minerals and water, NASA's MEGANE, which reads gamma rays and neutrons from cosmic-ray hits to count the elements in the ground, an ion analyser, a dust counter, a radiation monitor, and an 8K camera with Japan's broadcaster NHK. On the way out, it flies past Deimos, the smaller moon.

s12: So, the ledger. Known: the launch time, the rocket, the mass, the ten gram target, the three modules, the rover. Published plan: arrive 2027, rover 2029, capsule 2031. Open: which year the landing happens, whether it lands twice, how long it stays down, how much of Phobos is really Mars, and the big one, where Phobos came from. That last one is the whole point. The answer is in the dirt.

s13: What to watch: liftoff at 3:41 PM Eastern on October 19, boosters off two minutes in, and the moment seventy one minutes later when MMX is on its own. After that, a year of silence before Mars. We will be following it here, one real mission at a time. Space Now, from Heartbeat Observatory.
