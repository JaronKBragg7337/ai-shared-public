[s1] Think of the worst thing that ever happened to life on Earth. An asteroid? A volcano? There is a case that one of the biggest crises was caused by life itself, about 2.4 billion years ago, by a waste product: oxygen. This is Earth. Today: the Great Oxidation Event, what the rocks show, and what nobody can measure yet.
[s2] Today, oxygen is about 21 percent of the air. For roughly the first two billion years of Earth's history, it was almost absent. Chemistry in ancient rocks points to levels far below one thousandth of today's. Life was there, microbes in the oceans, but it lived in a world without free oxygen.
[s3] The source was a group of microbes called cyanobacteria. Their kind of photosynthesis splits water, and oxygen is the leftover. For them, it was waste. When this started is debated. Some evidence and genetic studies point to 2.7 billion years ago or earlier, and claims of even older fossils are contested. What the rocks agree on is this: the oxygen did not pile up in the air right away.
[s4] Why not right away? Because oxygen had places to go. Iron dissolved in the oceans grabbed it, and so did gases from volcanoes and freshly exposed rock. Only after those sinks were overwhelmed could free oxygen build up in the air. Estimates of that delay run from about 200 to 400 million years, and they depend on when the microbes started, which is not settled.
[s5] The oceans left a record in stone. Banded iron formations are rock layers that alternate rust-red iron minerals with grey silica. The great ones, like those in the Hamersley Range of Western Australia, are about 2.5 billion years old, and they hold most of the iron ore we mine today. The standard explanation: iron dissolved in oxygen-free seawater met oxygen, rusted, and sank in layers. But a few formed before oxygen was common, so other microbes or sunlight can also do it. The bands show iron leaving the ocean. On their own, they do not date the oxygen in the air.
[s6] The strongest clue is sulfur. In rocks older than about 2.4 billion years, sulfur carries an odd isotope pattern, called mass-independent fractionation, that forms when sunlight hits sulfur gases in air without oxygen. In 2000, Farquhar and colleagues showed it fades out of the rock record between about 2.45 and 2.09 billion years ago. Other clues agree. Grains of minerals that rust away, like uraninite and pyrite, wash into old river sediments, then stop appearing. Red, rusted soil layers show up. Still, this is a proxy. Rocks tell the story. Nobody measured the air.
[s7] So when? It depends who you ask. One 2017 study placed the start of the oxygen rise between 2.46 and 2.43 billion years ago. Other studies give ranges from about 2.5 to 2.2. It was probably not a switch. Oxygen likely rose and fell in pulses over a few hundred million years, and some estimates say it later reached up to about ten percent of today's level.
[s8] For microbes that had never met free oxygen, it was dangerous. Oxygen forms reactive molecules that can damage proteins, DNA, and cell membranes. Cells that evolved defenses, and then ways to use oxygen, got a big reward: breathing it releases many times more energy from the same food than fermenting does. Strict anaerobes did not vanish. Many still live today in mud, deep sediment, and animal guts, places oxygen does not reach. So for some, the story was retreat, not only death.
[s9] There is also a cold chapter. In rocks north of Lake Huron in Canada, geologists found three layers of glacial deposits, from roughly 2.45 to 2.22 billion years ago. One leading idea: the early air was warmed by methane, and oxygen destroyed methane, turning it into carbon dioxide, a weaker greenhouse gas. In South Africa, the Makganyene glaciation may have reached the tropics, a possible snowball Earth. But dating and matching these layers is disputed. One 2020 study found that oxygen rose before that snowball. That fits the idea, but the link is still a hypothesis.
[s10] Now the big question. Was it a mass extinction? Honestly: not established. Microbes leave few fossils, and rocks that old are rare and changed by heat and pressure. Nobody has counted species before and after. The idea rests on inference: oxygen was toxic, and oxygen-free habitats shrank. Some scientists call it Earth's first mass extinction, but it is usually left out of the standard lists. One study of sulfate minerals found a collapse in productivity of more than 80 percent. But that was about 2 billion years ago, at the end of an oxygen high, and it measures how much the biosphere produced, not how many species died.
[s11] Here is where things stand. Well supported: oxygen in the air rose sharply around 2.4 billion years ago, the sulfur record changed, and living things were the source. Debated: exactly when and how fast, when cyanobacteria began, whether this caused the ice ages, and how much life died. What would help: more precisely dated rocks, better chemical fingerprints of ancient microbes, and models that tie them together.
[s12] Life did not just live on Earth. It changed Earth: the air, the oceans, the rocks, and the climate. Everything that came after had to live in the world that life made. Every breath of oxygen began as somebody's waste.
