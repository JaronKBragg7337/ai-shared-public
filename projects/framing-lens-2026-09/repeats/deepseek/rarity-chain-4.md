There are a few possible answers, but the strongest one is **natural glass formed by a lightning strike** — specifically **fulgurite**.

Why it fits:

- **The ingredients are not rare.** Fulgurites are made mainly from **silica sand, soil, or rock**, which are extremely common on Earth.
- **It does not require exotic temperatures or pressures in principle.** Lightning produces high heat, but lightning itself is not rare globally.
- **The hard part is the full chain of conditions.** To make a natural fulgurite, you need:
  1. A thunderstorm,
  2. A lightning strike,
  3. That strike hitting a suitable sandy or siliceous ground material,
  4. Enough energy delivered in exactly the right way to melt/vitrify the material,
  5. Rapid cooling to preserve the glassy tube structure,
  6. And then the fragile structure must survive weathering and discovery.

So the material is rare not because its components are rare, but because the **process chain** is so contingent.

That said, a very common alternative answer is **tektite** or **meteoritic glass**, but those involve rare impact events and often exotic formation conditions. If the puzzle emphasizes “familiar, naturally occurring material on Earth” and “not rare ingredients, not narrow temperature/pressure alone, but the full chain,” **fulgurite** is the cleanest fit.

Another familiar candidate sometimes given is **Petrified wood**, but its ingredients and process are not quite as extreme or as universally rare.

So my answer would be: **fulgurite — natural glass made when lightning strikes sand or soil.** It is arguably among the rarest materials in the universe because the combination of a thunderstorm, a lightning strike, the right ground material, and post-strike preservation is an extraordinarily specific chain of events.
