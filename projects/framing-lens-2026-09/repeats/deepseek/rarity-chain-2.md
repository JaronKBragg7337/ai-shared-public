The material you’re likely thinking of is **petroleum / crude oil** — or more broadly, **fossil fuels** like coal, oil, and natural gas.

The argument goes like this:

- The **elements** in petroleum — carbon and hydrogen — are extremely common in the universe.
- It doesn’t require exotic temperatures or pressures in the way, say, diamonds or rare minerals do. Oil forms in relatively ordinary sedimentary basins.
- But to get **naturally occurring crude oil**, you need a very long and unlikely chain of conditions:
  1. Biological productivity — specifically, large amounts of plankton and other organic matter.
  2. Rapid burial in oxygen-poor sediments so the organic matter doesn’t fully decay.
  3. A geological basin that subsists long enough to bury and heat the material into kerogen and then oil.
  4. A source rock, reservoir rock, and cap rock arranged so the oil can migrate and be trapped rather than escape or degrade.
  5. Millions of years of stable geologic history, plate tectonics, and sedimentation.
  6. The oil must survive without being cooked into gas, biodegraded, or flushed away.

So while the ingredients are common and the physical conditions aren’t extreme, the **full chain of biological, sedimentary, tectonic, and temporal conditions** needed to produce crude oil is so elaborate that naturally occurring petroleum may be exceedingly rare in the universe. On Earth it’s familiar, but as a cosmic substance it could be argued to be among the rarest materials known — not because its atoms are rare, but because the process that assembles them into crude oil is so contingent.

Another common answer given to this kind of riddle is **coal**, for similar reasons: it requires ancient plant life, swampy burial, and long geological stability. But **petroleum** is usually the stronger example because it requires a more specific sequence of marine biological and geological conditions.
