This sounds like **wood** — or, more broadly, **timber from large trees**.

The argument is that the ingredients of wood (carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, etc.) are extremely common, and wood itself can form under a fairly wide range of temperatures and pressures. What is extraordinarily rare is the **full chain of conditions and processes** needed to produce it:

- a planet with liquid water and a suitable atmosphere
- stable, long-lived energy input from a star
- the origin of life
- the evolution of photosynthesis
- the evolution of land plants
- the evolution of vascular tissue
- the evolution of lignocellulose as a structural material
- the evolution of large, long-lived trees
- and then the specific ecological/geological circumstances that preserve it long enough for us to recognize it

In other words, wood is a biological material whose existence depends on a vast sequence of contingent evolutionary and planetary steps. Its chemical ingredients are common, and it doesn’t require exotic pressures or temperatures, but the **causal chain** required to make it is so long and specific that naturally occurring wood may be vastly rarer in the universe than many exotic minerals or metals.

A competing answer could be **fossil fuels** like coal, petroleum, or natural gas, since they also require a long chain involving life, burial, and geologic time. But the question says “familiar, naturally occurring material on Earth” and emphasizes the material itself being produced by nature, not just its ingredients being rare or its formation needing narrow conditions. That fit is especially strong for **wood**.

So my answer would be:

**Wood (especially from trees) — or lignocellulose.** It is a familiar natural material whose basic elements are common, but whose existence depends on an extremely long and unlikely chain of planetary, biological, and evolutionary conditions.
