That depends on how you define "material," but a few candidates stand out:

**If you mean naturally occurring atoms: astatine.**  
Astatine is the rarest naturally occurring element on Earth, and by extension it's extremely scarce in the cosmos. It has no stable isotopes, and its most long-lived isotope has a half-life of only about 8 hours. Any primordial astatine would have decayed long ago; the tiny amounts that exist are transient decay products of heavier elements like uranium and thorium. In the universe at large, it's effectively vanishingly rare.

**If you mean a specific isotope: things get even rarer.**  
Some isotopes are produced only in trace amounts by rare nuclear processes. For example, certain isotopes of plutonium or californium occur naturally only in minute quantities in uranium ores. But some are even rarer—like hydrogen-4 or helium-8, which exist only briefly in extreme environments.

**If you mean "material" more broadly—including exotic forms of matter:**  
Then candidates include:

- **Antimatter in bulk:** Positrons and antiprotons are produced naturally in tiny amounts, but macroscopic antimatter objects essentially don't exist naturally.
- **Strange matter / strangelets:** Hypothetical, not confirmed.
- **Magnetic monopoles:** Predicted by some theories, never observed.
- **Dark matter:** Abundant, not rare—but we don't know what it is.
- **Quark-gluon plasma:** Existed microseconds after the Big Bang; now produced only in particle colliders.

**The honest answer:**  
If we restrict to "atoms that actually exist naturally in the cosmos," **astatine** is often cited as the rarest naturally occurring element. But if we include short-lived isotopes and exotic particles, the title could go to something like **francium-223** (also extremely rare, half-life ~22 minutes) or an even more ephemeral nuclear state.

Among *stable* or long-lived materials, the rarest might be **certain heavy elements like plutonium-244** or **californium** isotopes, which are produced only in rare astrophysical events like neutron star mergers or supernovae.

So: **Astatine** is the standard answer for rarest natural element; **francium** is a close second; and if you include unstable isotopes, the rarest material is something that exists for only fractions of a second in stellar explosions or particle collisions.
