If I have to answer from reasoning alone, the honest answer is: **there isn’t a single known “rarest material” in any absolute sense** — because rarity depends on where you look, how you define “material,” and whether you mean naturally occurring or human-made.

That said, if you’re asking about **naturally occurring material in the cosmos**, the strongest candidates tend to be:

1. **Antimatter in bulk**  
   Antimatter is not rare in principle — it’s produced in particle collisions, cosmic rays, and radioactive decay. But **macroscopic amounts** of antimatter are extraordinarily rare. We’ve never observed a naturally occurring anti-asteroid, anti-planet, or anti-star. Even a single gram of antimatter would be a cosmological oddity.

2. **Elements beyond the periodic table’s natural range**  
   Superheavy elements like **oganesson** or hypothetical “island of stability” nuclei may exist only Fleetingly in laboratories or in violent astrophysical events. If any occur naturally, they’d likely decay almost instantly.

3. **Exotic matter / hypothetical substances**  
   Things like **magnetic monopoles**, **strangelet matter**, **dark matter particles**, or **quintessence** may be abundant in the universe, but if they exist, they’re not “materials” we can collect or even detect directly yet. If one specific exotic particle is extremely rare, that could be the true rarest thing — but we don’t know.

4. **Isotopes with extreme rarity**  
   Some isotopes are fantastically rare naturally. For example, **hydrogen-4**, **helium-8**, or certain transuranic isotopes may exist only in trace amounts. But “rarest material” usually implies a bulk substance, not a nuclear species.

5. **Meteoric/terrestrial rare substances**  
   Things like **quasicrystals** were once thought impossible in nature, then found in a Russian meteorite. **Lonsdaleite** (hexagonal diamond) is rare. But these are rare on Earth, not necessarily cosmos-wide.

**The deepest reasoning:**  
If the universe is infinite or sufficiently large, then **any physically possible material occurs somewhere, possibly infinitely many times.** In that case, “rarest” is either meaningless or refers to something with **zero abundance** — a material that is physically possible but never actually realized. That’s more of a philosophical category than a cosmic inventory item.

So my reasoned answer is:

> The rarest material in the cosmos is probably **bulk antimatter** — or possibly some **hypothetical exotic substance** like magnetic monopoles or strange matter — but we don’t actually know. If the universe is infinite, no physically allowed material is truly unique; only **zero-abundance materials** would be rarest.
