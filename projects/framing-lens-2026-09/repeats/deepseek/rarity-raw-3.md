That is a delightful question because the answer depends on how you define "material."

If by "material" you mean a naturally occurring chemical element, the rarest stable element in the universe is generally considered to be **astatine** (At). It has no stable isotopes at all — everything is radioactive with tiny half-lives — and it's estimated that less than a gram exists in Earth's entire crust at any given moment. In the cosmos at large, elements like plutonium, francium, and astatine are fantastically rare because they are produced only in trace amounts by rare nuclear processes.

If you mean the rarest *naturally occurring substance* in a broader sense, things get even more exotic. Some candidates:

- **Antimatter** — specifically antihelium or heavier antinuclei. The universe is overwhelmingly matter, and while positrons and antiprotons are produced in cosmic rays, complex antinuclei are almost nonexistent.
- **Quark-gluon plasma droplets** or **strange matter** — hypothetical, not confirmed.
- **Ultra-heavy elements** beyond the periodic table — if they exist at all, they'd be produced only in supernova or neutron-star collisions and decay almost instantly.

But if you want the single most defensible answer: **astatine** is the rarest naturally occurring element on Earth, and in the cosmos, elements like **francium** and **astatine** are the rarest *stable-ish* materials. However, if we include short-lived and exotic forms, the rarest material is probably **antimatter in bulk** — because in our universe, any significant clump of antimatter is essentially nonexistent.

So the best answer depends on scope:  
- Rarest natural element on Earth: **astatine**  
- Rarest element in the universe: likely **francium or astatine**, or possibly **plutonium-244** in interstellar space  
- Rarest material overall: **bulk antimatter** or **hypothetical exotic matter** like strangelets.

If you want a single crisp answer: **astatine** is the rarest naturally occurring element, and **antimatter** is the rarest form of matter in the cosmos.
