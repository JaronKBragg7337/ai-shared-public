That's a fun question because "rarest" depends on how you define it. Here are a few contenders, depending on the angle:

**By natural abundance in the universe:**
- **Astatine** is often cited as the rarest naturally occurring element on Earth, but in the cosmos, elements heavier than iron are all incredibly scarce because they require extreme events (supernovae, neutron star mergers) to forge.
- **Plutonium and other transuranics** occur only in trace amounts naturally.
- But the real winner might be **proton decay products** or **exotic matter** — if certain theories are right.

**By "does it exist at all":**
- **Anti-hydrogen** and other **antimatter** are extraordinarily rare in the cosmos — we've only detected tiny amounts.
- **Quark-gluon plasma** droplets might exist in exotic astrophysical conditions but are transient.
- **Magnetic monopoles** — if they exist — would be fantastically rare, possibly only a few in the entire observable universe.

**The strongest candidate:**
If we're talking about a material that we *know* exists but is almost impossibly scarce, **antimatter in bulk** (like anti-helium nuclei) is a strong contender. But if we allow hypothetical particles, **magnetic monopoles** or **Q-balls** could be the rarest.

**My pick for a concrete answer:**
**Antimatter — specifically, complex antinuclei like anti-helium-4 or heavier.** A few anti-helium nuclei have been detected in cosmic rays, but their abundance is mind-bogglingly tiny — perhaps a handful in the entire galaxy at any moment. In terms of a *material* you could point to, that's about as rare as it gets in the known cosmos.

If you want the single rarest *naturally occurring element* by cosmic abundance, it's probably **astatine** or **francium** — but those are Earth-centric answers. In the wider cosmos, heavy antinuclei win.
