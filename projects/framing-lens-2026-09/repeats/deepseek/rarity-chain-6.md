The material is **cellulose** — or more broadly, **wood** (and by extension, **coal**, since coal is fossilized wood/plant matter).

The argument goes like this:

- The **elements** in cellulose (carbon, hydrogen, oxygen) are extremely common.  
- The **temperature and pressure** conditions under which it forms are utterly ordinary — ambient conditions on Earth's surface.  
- What is extraordinarily rare is the **full chain of conditions and processes**: you need a planet with liquid water, a stable energy source (a star like the Sun), an atmosphere, and then — crucially — the emergence of **complex carbon-based biochemistry**, followed by the evolution of **photosynthetic multicellular life**, followed by the specific enzymatic machinery (cellulose synthase) to polymerize glucose into cellulose, and then the evolutionary development of **lignified vascular plants** that can stand upright and accumulate biomass.

Each step in that chain — abiogenesis, photosynthesis, the eukaryotic cell, multicellularity, land colonization, lignin biosynthesis — is itself a contingent, low-probability event. The product (wood/cellulose) is common on Earth only because we happen to live on the one planet where the entire chain succeeded. In the known universe, no other instance of this chain has been observed.

So while the **ingredients** are cosmically common and the **physical conditions** are mundane, the **causal history** required to assemble them into cellulose/wood is so long and contingent that it may be among the rarest types of material in the universe.

A close runner-up would be **limestone** (biogenic sedimentary rock), since it also requires biological processes (shells, coral) — but cellulose/wood is the stronger answer because it requires a longer and more specific evolutionary chain, including the emergence of land plants and lignin.
