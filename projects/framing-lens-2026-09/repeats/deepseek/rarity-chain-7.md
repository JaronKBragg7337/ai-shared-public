This is a classic “thinking outside the box” question. The answer is **natural glass**, specifically **obsidian** (or, more generally, naturally occurring glass like fulgurite, though obsidian is the usual target).

Here’s the reasoning:

- **Its ingredients are common.** Obsidian is mostly silica (SiO₂), one of the most abundant compounds in Earth’s crust and presumably in rocky planets everywhere.
- **Its formation window is not the issue.** It doesn’t require exotic pressures or temperatures; it forms when lava cools so quickly that atoms can’t arrange into a crystal lattice.
- **The rarity is in the full chain of conditions.** To get natural glass you need:
  1. A volcanic system with the right magma composition (high silica, high viscosity).
  2. An eruption that brings that magma to the surface.
  3. Rapid cooling — fast enough to prevent crystallization, but not so fast that it just shatters into ash.
  4. Subsequent preservation — obsidian is metastable; over geologic time it devitrifies (turns crystalline) or weathers away.
  5. The material must survive long enough to be found.

Each step is not terribly rare on its own, but the **combination** — a silica-rich volcano, an eruption, rapid quenching, and long-term survival — is a narrow coincidence. On a universal scale, most rocky bodies are basaltic or lack plate tectonics/water to drive explosive silicic volcanism. So while silica is everywhere, the **process chain** that yields obsidian is rare.

That’s why obsidian (or natural glass generally) is a familiar Earth material that can be argued to be among the rarest materials in the known universe — not because of its ingredients or its formation conditions individually, but because of the full sequence of events required to make it and then preserve it.
