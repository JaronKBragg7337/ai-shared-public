This is a fun question because "rarest" depends on how you define a material, but if we're talking about naturally occurring substances in the cosmos, the answer is almost certainly **antimatter** — specifically, complex antimatter atoms beyond simple antiprotons and positrons.

Here's the reasoning:

**Antimatter in general is extraordinarily rare.** The observable universe is overwhelmingly matter. For every billion or so matter particles created in the Big Bang, there was roughly one extra matter particle that survived — and essentially no primordial antimatter survived at all. The antimatter we do see is produced in tiny amounts by high-energy processes (cosmic rays, particle collisions, radioactive decay, etc.).

**But if we want the rarest *specific* material:**

- **Antihelium nuclei** — The AMS-02 experiment on the ISS has detected a handful of candidate antihelium events. Antihelium-3 and especially antihelium-4 are incredibly rare. Antihelium-4 has never been definitively confirmed in cosmic rays; even a single confirmed nucleus would be a major discovery. It's produced only in exotic processes and would be destroyed almost immediately upon contacting any normal matter.

- **Antihydrogen and heavier antiatoms** — These have been created in laboratories (CERN's ALPHA experiment made antihydrogen), but in the natural cosmos, they're essentially nonexistent in any stable form. Any antiatom that formed would annihilate with surrounding matter.

**Other contenders for "rarest natural material":**

- **Astable superheavy elements** — Elements beyond the periodic table we know, if they exist naturally, would be fantastically rare. But most are synthetic.

- **Quark-gluon plasma droplets** — Not really a "material" in the everyday sense.

- **Hypernuclei / strange matter** — Hypothetical, possibly not real.

- **Individual transuranic elements in nature** — Like naturally occurring plutonium-244 or californium, which exist only in trace amounts from natural nuclear processes.

**The strongest single answer:** If you mean a specific substance that we know exists but is vanishingly scarce, **antihelium-4** (or more generally, any complex antinucleus heavier than antihelium-3) is arguably the rarest material in the cosmos. Some physicists would say **antihydrogen gas** or **antihelium** more broadly. But if you're allowing purely theoretical substances, then **strangelet matter** or **magnetic monopoles** (if they exist) would be rarer still — possibly only a handful of particles in the entire observable universe.

So the cleanest answer: **Antimatter — especially complex antinuclei like antihelium — is the rarest naturally occurring material in the cosmos.**
