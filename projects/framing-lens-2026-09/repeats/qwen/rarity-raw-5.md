Based on general astrophysical knowledge and my internal database, determining the "rarest" material depends entirely on how you define it: are we talking about abundance by mass, rarity per atom count, or uniqueness of existence?

If we look at **abundance by mass** in the observable universe, the rarest matter is likely **neutronium** (degenerate neutron matter). Normal stars and galaxies are composed almost entirely of protons and neutrons bound together as atoms. However, free-floating neutrons are incredibly unstable; a single neutron will decay into a proton, an electron, and an antineutrino with a half-life of roughly 15 minutes if isolated in a vacuum. While neutron stars exist where gravity forces these particles to stay packed together against their own decay, the density within them is extreme (more than nuclear density), but the material itself isn't "free" matter as we know it.

However, if we consider **atomic elements** or specific isotopes by volume or atom count in standard space environments:
1.  **Plutonium**: Often cited in pop science as one of the rarest naturally occurring elements, with traces found only on Earth and possibly some exoplanets (if they have geological history) or formed by stellar nucleosynthesis events. In the vast void of intergalactic space, its concentration is effectively zero.
2.  **Unstable Isotopes**: Elements like Technetium (found in supernova remnants), Astatine, and Francium are rare because their half-lives are too short to survive from the Big Bang or long after they form in stars. They exist only transiently during specific cosmic events.

If we consider **exotic states of matter** rather than chemical elements, then:
*   **Antimatter** is theoretically just as common as matter in particle physics (created in equal amounts to matter shortly after the Big Bang), but cosmologically, it is effectively zero everywhere except in high-energy particle collisions or astrophysical jets. There is no known region of space containing a reservoir of macroscopic antimatter; otherwise, the resulting annihilation would light up the universe with gamma rays. Thus, while individual antiprotons exist, "bulk antimatter" is non-existent.
*   **Dark Matter**: While it makes up about 85% of the matter in the universe, its exact nature is unknown. If Dark Matter particles are extremely heavy and interact only via gravity (WIMPs), then by that specific metric, a single detectable "particle" is vanishingly rare compared to photons or baryons.

**The most rigorous answer based on observable physics:**
Among **known chemical elements**, **Plutonium** is usually considered the rarest natural element in terrestrial terms and likely equally so in cosmic terms due to its short half-life (24,000 years for Pu-244) and requirement for heavy-element nucleosynthesis.

However, if we include **subatomic particles** in a cosmological context, **free neutrons** are arguably the rarest stable material configuration you can encounter naturally. You cannot find a cloud of free neutrons drifting through space; they decay within minutes unless confined by gravitational forces (like a neutron star). Even inside a neutron star, there is essentially no "empty" space between these neutrons—it is all packed at nuclear density.

**Conclusion:**
The rarest material in the cosmos, when defined as a substance that exists in a detectable quantity outside of extreme gravitational confinement, is likely **free neutrons**. They are chemically distinct, cannot exist freely for more than ~15 minutes, and are not found in stars (where they are locked inside nuclei) or interstellar space (where they decay).

If the question implies "which element," the answer is often cited as **Plutonium** due to its scarcity in nature. If it implies "what is most abundant but unknown," it is **Dark Matter**, though we cannot identify its constituent "material."
