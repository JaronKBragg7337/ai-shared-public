Based on the constraints you provided—a material that is familiar, naturally occurring on Earth, yet arguably among the rarest in the universe not due to the scarcity of its atomic ingredients (oxygen and silicon are abundant), nor simply due to a narrow temperature window at a single point in time, but because of the **full chain of conditions and processes** required—the strongest candidate is **granite**.

Here is the reasoning for this conclusion:

### 1. The Ingredients Are Not Rare
Granite is composed primarily of quartz (silicon dioxide), feldspar, and mica. Silicon and oxygen are the two most abundant elements in the universe and on Earth. Therefore, the scarcity cannot be attributed to the availability of raw atomic building blocks. If you had infinite silicon and oxygen scattered randomly throughout space, they would not form granite.

### 2. It Is Not Defined by a Single Temperature/Pressure Point
While granitoid melting does occur within specific temperature and pressure ranges (the solidus and liquidus lines in phase diagrams), arguing that granite is "rare" because it only forms at those exact temperatures ignores the fact that the **process** is dynamic. A rock can remain granitic over a wide volume if it cools slowly enough, or it can be metamorphosed from granite under different conditions (becoming gneiss or schist). The rarity lies not in the specific thermodynamic coordinates, but in the successful completion of the entire evolutionary cycle to produce this specific stable solid-state assemblage.

### 3. The Full Chain of Conditions and Processes
Granite formation requires a unique and statistically improbable sequence of geological events known as **granitogenesis**:
*   **Differentiation:** It requires a large, deep magma chamber where molten rock separates from lighter silicate minerals (which float) and heavier minerals (which sink). This process, called fractional crystallization, is rare because it takes place in very deep crustal environments.
*   **Mantle or Deep Crustal Origin:** The melt usually originates from the partial melting of old, subducted oceanic crust or the underlying mantle. This requires plate tectonics, specifically subduction zones, which are geologically complex and limited to specific regions of Earth's history.
*   **Slow Cooling:** For crystals to grow large enough to form the distinct texture of granite (rather than fine-grained basalt or microscopic glass), the magma must cool extremely slowly over hundreds of thousands to millions of years.
*   **Solidification and Exposure:** The molten rock must eventually intrude into the crust, crystallize entirely, and then be uplifted by further tectonic movement to reach the surface, where it is exposed as a mountain range.

### 4. Why This Makes It Universally Rare
The "full chain" argument holds because **plate tectonics** itself is unique in our current understanding of planetary science. Almost no other body in the solar system (let alone the wider universe) has shown evidence of active plate tectonics capable of cycling deep mantle material up to the crust and back down repeatedly over billions of years.

Consequently:
*   **Space:** While silicon dioxide exists in asteroids and meteorites, they generally formed from rapid cooling or different chemical environments and rarely show the specific texture and mineralogical equilibrium of granite.
*   **Earth:** Granite covers only about 20% of the continental crust by area (roughly 1/5th). Compared to the entire volume of the Earth's interior, which is mostly iron-nickel alloy or basaltic mantle material, granite is a very small fraction.
*   **Probability:** In a universe populated by billions of exoplanets, only those with active plate tectonics and sufficient time for deep differentiation can produce granitic terrain.

### Conclusion
**Granite** fits your description perfectly. It is a common building material on Earth, composed of elements that are abundant everywhere in the cosmos. However, its existence requires the rare conjunction of a planetary system capable of subduction, the generation of deep-mantle magmas, the time required for slow crystallization over geological epochs, and tectonic uplift. The probability of a specific planet developing this full evolutionary chain to create granite is arguably infinitesimally small compared to the probability of simply finding silicon and oxygen floating in a nebula.
