Based on the criteria provided—familiarity to humans, natural occurrence on Earth, extreme rarity in the universe (not due to ingredient scarcity or specific reaction conditions), and a reliance on a complex biological chain—the strongest candidate is **freshwater** (specifically liquid water contained within large bodies).

Here is the reasoning for why this fits:

1.  **Natural Occurrence:** Water ($H_2O$) is ubiquitous on Earth in its oceans, lakes, and rivers.
2.  **Ingredient Availability:** Hydrogen and oxygen are incredibly common elements in the universe; stars constantly forge hydrogen and fuse helium into heavier elements, eventually creating conditions where water forms. There is no lack of raw material in cosmic dust or stellar atmospheres to make water molecules.
3.  **Temperature/Pressure Independence:** While Earth's surface has a specific temperature range, the physics of water formation doesn't require a "narrow window." In fact, water condenses and exists as ice or vapor over vast ranges of temperature and pressure found throughout interstellar clouds. Stars have likely produced billions of tons of water that simply never condensed into liquid due to gravity or radiation.
4.  **The Complex Biological Chain (The Key Factor):** The argument for its "rarity" lies entirely in the transition from an isolated water molecule to a stable, large reservoir of **liquid freshwater**.
    *   In the early solar system and elsewhere, water was likely abundant as vapor or ice in space. It did not need biology to form; gravity and thermodynamics handled that.
    *   The transformation into **freshwater**—driven by a continuous cycle of evaporation, condensation, and collection due to Earth's specific planetary rotation (centrifugal force shaping clouds), its magnetic field protecting it, and most crucially, the biosphere actively cycling it—requires an active, self-sustaining planetary system.
    *   Nature does not spontaneously organize into a single, massive body of fresh liquid water anywhere else in the known universe simply because atoms collide to form $H_2O$. That specific state requires the precise engineering of a planet with the right heat gradient and atmospheric dynamics.

A similar, perhaps more extreme, argument could be made for **freshwater icebergs** or **glaciers**, but these are subsets of liquid water systems (which freeze). However, if we consider **living organisms** themselves as the material:
*   Life is naturally occurring on Earth.
*   The ingredients (C, H, O, N, P, S) are common in space; the Big Bang and Star evolution provided them abundantly.
*   It does not require a narrow temperature or pressure range to *form* in theory (abiogenesis can happen under various conditions).
*   However, the probability of abiogenesis occurring spontaneously anywhere else is infinitesimally small because it relies on the full chain: stellar nucleosynthesis $\rightarrow$ accumulation in solar system $\rightarrow$ delivery to a planetary body $\rightarrow$ acquisition of liquid water $\rightarrow$ chemical complexity $\rightarrow$ self-replicating molecules $\rightarrow$ natural selection.

**Conclusion:**
While "liquid freshwater" is the most common *form* of water on Earth that requires the active planet to maintain, the material that best fits the description of a complex biological necessity resulting from a full chain of rare cosmic events is **life itself**.

However, if we stick strictly to an inorganic "material" (a substance rather than a system), the argument for **freshwater** remains the most logical. It is made of elements that are everywhere in the universe, formed by processes common in stars and planetary formation, yet we possess only two relatively small bodies of it on Earth, and none exist elsewhere known. The "rare" aspect is not the chemistry of water, but the specific **hydrological cycle** required to keep it liquid and fresh in a single location.

Therefore, the answer is **Freshwater** (or more broadly, **Liquid Water in planetary reservoirs**). It argues that while you can make a bottle of water in a lab with basic chemistry, you cannot make *Earth's freshwater* without the entire evolutionary history of our planet forming, orbiting the Sun, and developing its specific atmospheric dynamics.
