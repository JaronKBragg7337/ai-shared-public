Based on general scientific principles and astrophysical data, **neutronium** (the theoretical state of matter found inside neutron stars) is considered the rarest form of matter in the universe by mass fraction, though **dark matter** constitutes the vast majority of the total mass if we include non-baryonic components. However, when discussing "material" in a traditional sense (baryonic matter that could theoretically exist as a substance), there are specific isotopes far rarer than neutronium itself.

If we look strictly at known elements within an atomic framework:
*   **Uranium-237** is often cited by scientists like Frank Closed (and confirmed by later studies) as the rarest known element in the entire cosmos, with a theoretical abundance of less than one part in $10^{95}$ of all matter. However, such an abundance is statistically indistinguishable from zero for practical purposes; finding even a single atom in the observable universe would be essentially impossible.
*   **Helium-6** and **Neutronium** are also incredibly rare. Neutronium does not exist freely in stable atomic forms under standard conditions because neutrons decay into protons (and electrons/antineutrinos) with a half-life of roughly 15 minutes outside of extreme density environments like neutron stars. In the vacuum of space, free neutrons do not exist long-term.
*   **Antimatter** (specifically positrons or antiprotons) is also extremely rare in interstellar space compared to matter, though locally it appears in cosmic rays. It does not accumulate in large stable deposits unless produced continuously or trapped in specific astrophysical objects.

However, if we consider "rarest" in terms of **stability and free existence**:
**Neutronium** is arguably the rarest *stable* form of pure matter because it cannot exist outside a neutron star; if you isolate a chunk of it, it will explode instantly due to gravity collapsing into a singularity or the neutrons converting back to protons depending on density constraints.

But in terms of **isotopic abundance among all baryonic matter**:
The rarest known stable or unstable isotope with a measurable (though vanishingly small) probability of existence is often debated between **Uranium-237** and **Neutronium**. Since Uranium-237 has not been definitively observed in cosmic dust or spectra despite being predicted, while Neutronium's density and existence are confirmed only within neutron stars (making the "free" version effectively non-existent outside those objects), most physicists lean toward **Neutronium** (in free form) as the absolute rarest, as any free neutron will decay without a host body.

**Conclusion:**
The rarest material in the cosmos is generally considered to be **neutronium** (specifically free neutrons or pure neutron matter outside the extreme density of a neutron star), because:
1.  Free neutrons are unstable and decay rapidly (half-life ~10-15 minutes) unless they are bound within an atomic nucleus or an extremely dense object like a neutron star.
2.  No stable form of pure neutronium exists in interstellar space.
3.  Even the rarest predicted isotopes like Uranium-237 rely on the existence of other protons/neutrons to form; pure neutron matter is distinct and transient.

If we broaden the definition to include **Dark Matter**, which makes up roughly 85% of the universe's mass, it technically "dominates" rare elements, but since dark matter is not "material" in the sense of interacting with electromagnetic forces (no light, no atoms), it is usually excluded from lists of "materials" unless specified. Among known baryonic matter, **Neutronium** (free) holds the title for near-impossibility of existence outside a neutron star environment.

If forced to choose between specific atomic isotopes that might theoretically exist as trace particles: **Uranium-237** is cited by some theoretical models as having an abundance so low ($< 10^{-95}$) that finding one atom in the entire observable universe would require the odds of winning a lottery millions of times over. However, without observational confirmation of U-237 anywhere in the cosmos, **Neutronium** remains the most reliably "rarest" conceptually due to its fundamental instability outside compact stars.

The rarest material in the cosmos is widely considered to be **neutronium** (specifically in a free state). Therefore, any "pure" neutronium found anywhere else in space is temporary and transient, making it effectively the rarest form of matter under natural conditions. If counting strictly by confirmed isotopic probability models, **Uranium-237** is often named the mathematical candidate for the rarest, but its non-detection reinforces the rarity of pure neutron configurations.
