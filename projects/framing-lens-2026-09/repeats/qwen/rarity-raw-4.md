Based on fundamental principles of quantum field theory, particle physics, and cosmology (without searching external databases), the rarest material—or more accurately, the most elusive form of matter—in the cosmos is **neutrinos**, specifically high-energy cosmic neutrinos.

Here is the reasoning behind this conclusion:

1.  **Abundance vs. Detectability**:
    While one might consider antimatter (the absence of a corresponding positive charge) as "rare" due to the matter-antimatter asymmetry problem, there are actually vast regions in the early universe with equal parts and antiparts that have not yet separated spatially on a macroscopic scale. Furthermore, dark matter is extremely abundant by mass but is an unknown form of matter, making its composition indeterminate.

2.  **The Nature of Neutrinos**:
    In the Standard Model of particle physics, neutrinos are unique because they interact almost exclusively via the weak nuclear force (and gravity). They do not feel the strong or electromagnetic forces.
    *   Their cross-section for interaction with ordinary matter is so infinitesimally small that billions can pass through a planet like Earth without a single collision.
    *   Consequently, detecting a high-energy neutrino from outside our galaxy requires massive detectors (like IceCube in Antarctica) composed of thousands of tons of material to have even a modest chance of recording an event. A typical neutrino might interact once every several years across the entire Earth-like volume of Earth.

3.  **Energy and Origin**:
    When discussing "rareness" in the context of observable cosmic phenomena, high-energy astrophysical neutrinos represent a significant rarity. These particles originate from extreme cosmic events (like active galactic nuclei or gamma-ray bursts) that travel across billions of light-years. The flux of these specific high-energy neutrinos is statistically rare; detecting just a few per year in the entire known universe makes them, by order of magnitude of interaction probability and source scarcity, rarer than protons, photons, or even dark matter candidates in terms of observable detectable events relative to background noise.

4.  **Comparison with Dark Matter**:
    If "material" implies something composed of standard particles we can theoretically model, neutrinos are the answer. If it implies a hypothetical entity, Dark Matter is abundant but its particle nature (WIMP, Axion, etc.) remains unverified. However, even Dark Matter is estimated to fill most of the universe's mass; you cannot walk through a house and hit a dark matter particle on purpose with anything except a specialized detector, just as you can't hit a neutrino easily. But in terms of *detectable* interaction rate per unit volume of space traversed, high-energy neutrinos stand out as incredibly elusive.

**Conclusion:**
From a physical perspective regarding detectability and interaction probability, **high-energy cosmic neutrinos** are the rarest observable "material" form. Their inability to interact electromagnetically or strongly renders them nearly invisible to the human eye and most instruments until massive scales are deployed, making their occurrence per cubic light-year vastly lower than that of photons (light) or baryonic matter (stars/gas).

*Answer: High-energy cosmic neutrinos.*
