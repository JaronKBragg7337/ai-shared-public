• **Wood** is a strong candidate—specifically, the lignified cellular tissue produced by trees.

  Its ingredients are not cosmically scarce: carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen are abundant, and wood forms under comparatively ordinary temperatures and pressures. What is extraordinarily demanding is the **process that makes wood wood**:

  - a suitable planet with liquid water and a long-lived, relatively stable environment;
  - the origin and persistence of life;
  - photosynthesis and the accumulation of atmospheric oxygen;
  - the evolution of complex multicellular organisms and land plants;
  - the evolution of vascular tissue, cellulose, lignin, and secondary growth;
  - living cells constructing those substances into wood’s intricate, organized structure.

  Simply assembling the right chemicals would not reproduce natural wood, because its cellular architecture is part of the material. It is the product of an entire evolutionary and ecological history, not merely a chemical recipe.

  This also captures why wood can plausibly be called rarer than diamond: carbon crystals can arise through several nonbiological processes, whereas every known piece of wood required life and a very particular sequence of evolutionary developments. Coal, amber, bone, and other biological materials could support similar arguments, but wood is the especially vivid everyday example.

  Strictly speaking, “rarest in the universe” is not a measurable ranking without a cosmic inventory. It is best understood as an argument about **contingent complexity**: abundant ingredients, ordinary physical conditions, but an exceptionally improbable natural production history.
