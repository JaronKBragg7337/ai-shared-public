There isn't a single agreed answer, because it depends on what you count as a "material."

**Rarest naturally occurring element: astatine.** Element 85 is usually given this title. Its longest-lived isotope has a half-life of only about 8 hours, and it's produced only as a short-lived link in the decay chains of uranium and thorium. The entire Earth's crust probably holds less than a gram of it at any moment. Francium is similarly fleeting. Both are probably vanishingly rare everywhere, since the same decay processes would produce them anywhere uranium and thorium exist.

**Rarest in terms of cosmic abundance.** Among elements that are stable or long-lived, beryllium and boron are strikingly scarce for their light weight. The Big Bang made almost none, and stellar fusion skips over them, so they come mostly from cosmic-ray spallation. Technetium and promethium have no stable isotopes and appear only in trace amounts, though technetium has been detected in some red giant stars.

**Rarest if you include exotic matter.** Antimatter is the standout. Apart from tiny amounts in cosmic rays, lightning, and radioactive decays, the universe seems to contain almost none. Anti-helium nuclei are especially rare. Hypothetical forms such as strange quark matter might exist in neutron star cores, but that isn't confirmed.

**Man-made elements** like oganesson don't count as natural, though some heavy ones may form briefly in neutron-star mergers.

If you want a single answer, I'd say astatine for natural matter and antimatter (especially anti-nuclei heavier than hydrogen) for the universe as a whole.
