**Granite** is probably the best candidate, along with the continental crust it makes up.

Its ingredients are not rare. Silicon, oxygen, aluminum, sodium, potassium and calcium are common everywhere, and the minerals involved (quartz, feldspars, micas) form at ordinary crustal temperatures and pressures. The rarity comes from the long chain of events needed to produce it:

1. **A rocky planet** with enough internal heat and the right composition to differentiate into core, mantle and crust.
2. **Abundant liquid water** at the surface, which hydrates basaltic oceanic crust.
3. **Plate tectonics**, which drags that wet crust down in subduction zones.
4. **Water-fluxed partial melting.** Water released from the sinking slab lowers the melting point of the mantle above it, producing more silica-rich magmas.
5. **Repeated remelting and fractional crystallization**, over hundreds of millions to billions of years, which gradually concentrate silica and the other elements that favor granite.
6. **Long-term stability**, so the buoyant granitic crust accumulates as continents instead of being recycled back into the mantle.

Most rocky bodies we know of, such as the Moon, Mars, Mercury and Venus, are dominated by basalt and lack large amounts of true granite. Earth is the only place where we know it is abundant, and its formation depends on a planet having water, tectonics and a very long history of crustal recycling all at once.

Other materials could make a similar argument, such as limestone, wood and amber, which depend on life. Granite is the clearest case where the rarity comes from planetary processes rather than from rare elements or extreme conditions.
