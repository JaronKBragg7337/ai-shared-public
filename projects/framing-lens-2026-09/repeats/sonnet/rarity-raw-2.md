There's no single settled answer, because it depends on what you count as a "material."

- **Rarest naturally occurring element: astatine.** It's usually the one named. All its isotopes are radioactive and short-lived (the longest has a half-life of about 8 hours). It exists only as a fleeting product of uranium and thorium decay chains. Estimates suggest less than a gram exists in Earth's crust at any moment, and it should be similarly scarce wherever else heavy elements decay.

- **Rarest synthetic element: oganesson (element 118)** and its neighbors. Only a handful of atoms have ever been made, each lasting for milliseconds or less. They may also form briefly in extreme events like neutron-star mergers, but nothing suggests they exist in meaningful quantity anywhere.

- **Rarest stable elements:** Cosmically, lithium, beryllium, and boron are scarce because the Big Bang and stellar fusion largely skip over them. They're made mainly by cosmic-ray spallation. Beryllium is probably the rarest of the stable ones. Among heavy stable elements, things like thulium and lutetium are the least abundant, along with the heaviest r-process products.

- **Rarest if you include antimatter:** Antimatter is extremely scarce in the observable universe. Natural antihelium, if it exists at all, would be rarer still. Antimatter made in labs, such as antihydrogen, comes in quantities of a few atoms at a time.

So if I had to pick one: **astatine** among natural materials, and **antimatter** if you broaden the question to all forms of matter.
