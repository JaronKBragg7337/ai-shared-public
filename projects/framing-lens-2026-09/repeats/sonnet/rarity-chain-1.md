My best candidate is **granite**, and the continental crust it makes up.

Its ingredients are not rare: silicon, oxygen, aluminum, sodium, potassium and the like are abundant everywhere. The pressures and temperatures at which granite crystallizes are also unremarkable. What is unusual is the long sequence of things that has to happen first:

1. **A rocky planet with liquid water at the surface and in the crust.** Water-rich sediments and altered basalt are needed as starting material.
2. **Plate tectonics, especially subduction.** Water-laden oceanic crust is dragged down, and the water lowers the melting point of the mantle and crust above it.
3. **Repeated partial melting and re-melting.** This is not a single melting event. Basalt melts to give more silica-rich magma, and older crust and sediments are re-melted again and again.
4. **Fractional crystallization and differentiation.** Slow cooling in magma chambers over millions of years sorts the melt into progressively more silica-rich, light compositions.
5. **Enough time and continued heat.** The planet has to stay geologically active for billions of years.

Most rocky bodies never get through this chain. The Moon, Mercury, Mars and the asteroids are dominated by basalt and similar primitive rocks. Venus has little or no water and no plate tectonics. As far as we know, Earth is the only place with large amounts of granite.

Other candidates exist, such as limestone, coal, wood and amber. These depend on life, which adds its own chain of improbable conditions. Granite is the clearest case of a common-looking rock whose existence depends on a whole planetary history.
