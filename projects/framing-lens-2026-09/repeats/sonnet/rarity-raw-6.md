There's no single agreed answer, because it depends on what you mean by "material."

**Rarest naturally occurring element: astatine.** Astatine is usually given this title. Its longest-lived isotope has a half-life of about 8 hours, and it only appears as a short-lived link in the decay chains of uranium and thorium. Earth's crust likely holds less than a gram of it at any moment. Francium is similarly fleeting, though it is probably a bit more abundant.

**Rarest among elements made in stars and the Big Bang:** Beryllium, boron, and lithium are unusually scarce for their light weights. Stellar fusion tends to destroy or skip over them rather than build them. Cosmic-ray collisions produce much of the beryllium and boron we find. Among heavier elements, the ones made only by rare events like neutron star mergers, such as gold, platinum, and the heaviest r-process elements, are also quite scarce.

**Rarest if you include synthetic or exotic matter:**
- **Superheavy elements** like oganesson and tennessine have only ever been made a few atoms at a time, and they decay in milliseconds. They likely don't exist naturally anywhere, or only briefly in extreme events.
- **Antimatter** is rare in our universe. Natural antimatter shows up only in trace amounts, in cosmic rays, lightning, and radioactive decays. Antihelium nuclei are especially hard to produce and detect.
- **Hypothetical matter**, such as magnetic monopoles, strange quark matter, or certain dark matter candidates, may be rarer still, or may not exist at all.

So the most defensible answer is astatine for natural matter, with antimatter and superheavy elements as contenders if you count what's made in labs or only exists fleetingly.
