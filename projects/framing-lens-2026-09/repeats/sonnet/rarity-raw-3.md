There's no definitive answer, because it depends on what you count as a "material," but the best candidate is **astatine**.

- **Astatine (element 85)** is generally considered the rarest naturally occurring element. All its isotopes are radioactive, and the longest-lived has a half-life of only about 8 hours. It exists only as a fleeting intermediate in the decay chains of uranium and thorium. Estimates suggest less than a gram exists in Earth's crust at any moment, and the same logic suggests it is vanishingly scarce throughout the cosmos.

Other contenders, depending on how you define it:

- **Promethium and technetium** are also extremely scarce naturally, since they have no long-lived isotopes. Technetium has been detected in some stars, though, where it is produced on the spot.
- **Transuranic elements** such as berkelium, californium, and beyond are essentially absent in nature today. They are probably made briefly in neutron-star mergers or supernovae, and otherwise only in labs and reactors.
- **Antimatter** is extremely rare in the observable universe, apart from tiny amounts from cosmic-ray collisions, radioactive decays, and lightning. It is arguably the rarest "stuff," but it's not an element.
- **Hypothetical exotica** such as magnetic monopoles, strange matter, or primordial relics may be even rarer, or may not exist at all.

If you mean ordinary, confirmed matter that occurs naturally, astatine is the usual answer.
