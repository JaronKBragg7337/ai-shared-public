There's no single agreed answer, because it depends on what you mean by "material."

**Rarest naturally occurring element: astatine.** Astatine (element 85) is usually named the rarest. All its isotopes are radioactive, and the longest-lived has a half-life of only about 8 hours. It forms only as a fleeting link in the decay chains of uranium and thorium. Estimates suggest less than a gram exists in Earth's crust at any moment. Francium is similarly ephemeral, with a half-life of about 22 minutes.

**Rare among the stable light elements: lithium, beryllium, and boron.** The Big Bang made little lithium, and stellar fusion mostly skips over or destroys these three elements. They are far scarcer than the elements around them, such as helium, carbon, and oxygen.

**Rarest matter overall: antimatter.** Apart from tiny amounts from cosmic-ray collisions, radioactive decays, and lightning, antimatter is almost absent from the observable universe. Making it in the lab, even a few atoms of antihydrogen, takes enormous effort. Antimatter is arguably the rarest "stuff," and why matter dominates is one of physics' big unsolved questions.

**Hypothetical candidates.** Magnetic monopoles and strange quark matter may be extremely rare or may not exist at all.

So the usual answer is astatine for natural elements, and antimatter if you count any kind of matter.
