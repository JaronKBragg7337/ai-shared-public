There's no single agreed answer because it depends on how you define "material," but here are the best candidates.

**Astatine** is the usual answer for the rarest naturally occurring element. It's a radioactive halogen whose longest-lived isotopes have half-lives of only hours. It's constantly produced in tiny amounts by the decay of uranium and thorium and then vanishes just as quickly. Estimates suggest less than a gram exists in Earth's crust at any moment. Francium is similarly scarce.

**Synthetic superheavy elements** are rarer still. Elements like tennessine and oganesson have been made only a few atoms at a time in particle accelerators, and they last milliseconds or less. Natural processes, such as neutron-star mergers, might make them briefly, but they don't persist.

**Antimatter** is arguably the rarest substance on a cosmic scale. The universe is overwhelmingly matter. Antimatter shows up only in trace amounts, from cosmic-ray collisions, certain radioactive decays, and lightning or other high-energy events. Anything heavier than antiprotons or antihelium nuclei is essentially absent in nature, and the ones made in labs amount to a few atoms.

Among **stable** elements, the rarest are heavy ones made only by rare events like neutron-star mergers, along with fragile light elements such as beryllium and boron, which are destroyed in stars and only made by cosmic-ray spallation.

So: **astatine** for natural elements, and **antimatter** if you count the cosmos as a whole.
