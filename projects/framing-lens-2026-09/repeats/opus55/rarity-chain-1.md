# Granite

Granite is everywhere on Earth: countertops, mountain ranges, the bulk of the continents. Yet a good case can be made that it is one of the rarest rock types we know of. Geologists Ian Campbell and Stuart Ross Taylor summed this up in their 1983 paper, *"No water, no granites – no oceans, no continents."*

## Why the ingredients aren't the problem
Granite is mostly quartz, feldspar and mica. These are built from oxygen, silicon, aluminum, potassium and sodium, which are among the most common rock-forming elements in any rocky planet. It also crystallizes at ordinary crustal temperatures and pressures. Nothing about its chemistry or physics is exotic.

## Why it's rare: the chain of required processes
Planets don't make granite directly. The default products of melting a rocky planet's mantle are basalt and similar rocks, which is what we see on the Moon, Mars, Venus, Mercury and the asteroids. Getting to granite requires a multi-step history:

1. **A differentiated rocky planet** with an active, hot interior that keeps producing basaltic crust.
2. **Abundant liquid water at the surface**, sustained over geological time. This requires the right distance from the star, enough mass to hold an atmosphere, and so on.
3. **Water-altered crust**, where basalt reacts with seawater to form hydrated minerals.
4. **A recycling mechanism, essentially plate tectonics and subduction**, that carries that wet crust back down into the mantle.
5. **Water-assisted partial melting.** Water lowers melting points and changes which components melt, producing silica-rich magmas instead of more basalt.
6. **Repeated cycles of melting, separation and re-melting** over hundreds of millions to billions of years, progressively distilling more silica- and potassium-rich melts.
7. **Slow cooling deep in thick crust**, so the magma crystallizes as coarse-grained granite rather than erupting.

## The evidence
- Earth is the only body known to have large volumes of granite and true continental crust.
- The Moon has only trace granitic fragments.
- Mars and Venus show at most small or debated felsic patches.
- Plate tectonics with surface oceans is, as far as we know, unique to Earth.

So granite is rare not because of what it's made of, but because it requires a planet that has had liquid oceans and active tectonic recycling for a very long time. In that sense, the continents we stand on are themselves an unusual planetary feature.

Similar arguments can be made for materials that need life, such as soil, chalk and limestone, coal, or oil. Granite is the classic example, though, because it needs no biology at all, only water and tectonics.
