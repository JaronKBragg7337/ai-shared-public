There isn't one definitive answer because "rarest" depends on what you count, but here are the main contenders.

**Synthetic superheavy elements (rarest things that definitely exist)**
Elements like **oganesson (118)** and **tennessine (117)** have only been made a few atoms at a time in particle accelerators. Only around five or six oganesson atoms have ever been confirmed, and each lasted under a millisecond. They might form briefly in neutron star mergers, but there's no evidence they persist anywhere. By total quantity in the universe at any moment, they're arguably the rarest known substances.

**Rarest naturally occurring element**
**Astatine** usually takes this title. It forms briefly in the decay chains of uranium and thorium and has a half-life of hours at most. Estimates suggest less than about a gram exists in Earth's crust at any given time. **Francium** is a close runner-up for the same reason.

**Rarest stable elements cosmically**
Among elements that don't decay, heavy ones like **rhenium, thulium, lutetium, and tantalum** are among the least abundant in the universe. They're made only in rare stellar events like supernovae and neutron star collisions.

**Antimatter**
On a cosmic scale, antimatter is strikingly rare. The early universe should have produced matter and antimatter in nearly equal amounts, yet almost all antimatter vanished. Today it appears only fleetingly, in cosmic ray collisions, radioactive decay, and labs. Why this imbalance exists is one of physics' big open questions.

**Minerals**
If you mean naturally formed minerals, **kyawthuite** from Myanmar is often called the rarest. Only a single crystal is known.

**Hypothetical materials**
Things like magnetic monopoles or stable "strange matter" might be even rarer, possibly nonexistent, since none has ever been detected.

**Short answer:** Among real, confirmed substances, superheavy synthetic elements like oganesson are probably the rarest. The rarest natural element is astatine.
