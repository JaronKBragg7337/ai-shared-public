There's no single answer, because it depends on what you count as a "material." Here are the main contenders:

**Naturally occurring elements**
- **Astatine** is usually called the rarest naturally occurring element on Earth. Less than about a gram exists in the crust at any moment, because it forms only briefly in the decay chains of uranium and thorium and then decays quickly.
- **Francium** is a close runner-up for the same reason.
- Across the universe as a whole, these short-lived elements are vanishingly rare, since they exist only fleetingly wherever heavier radioactive elements are decaying.

**Rarest primordial isotope**
- **Tantalum-180m** is a metastable isomer that has survived since before the Solar System formed. It is the rarest primordial nuclide and the only naturally occurring nuclear isomer that persists in measurable amounts.

**Synthetic superheavy elements**
- Elements like **oganesson (118)** or **tennessine (117)** have been made only a handful of atoms at a time in particle accelerators, and they last milliseconds. They might form momentarily in neutron-star mergers, but in practice they are about as rare as matter gets.

**Antimatter**
- Almost all antimatter that should have formed in the Big Bang annihilated, leaving a small surplus of ordinary matter. Today antimatter appears only briefly, from cosmic rays, radioactive decay, and high-energy astrophysical events. Bulk antimatter is essentially absent from the observable universe.

**Hypothetical exotic matter**
- Things like **magnetic monopoles** or **strange matter** have never been confirmed to exist at all. If they exist, they would be extraordinarily rare. If they don't, their abundance is zero.

**Bottom line:** For a confirmed natural element, astatine is the usual answer. For a rare isotope, tantalum-180m. If you include artificial elements, the superheavies like oganesson win. In the broadest cosmic sense, antimatter is strikingly scarce given how much of it the early universe should have contained.
