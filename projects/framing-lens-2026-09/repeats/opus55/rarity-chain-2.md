# Granite

Granite is the strongest candidate. It is one of the most ordinary rocks on Earth: countertops, mountains, building stone, the bulk of the continents. Yet geologists Ian Campbell and Stuart Ross Taylor argued in a 1983 paper titled "No water, no granites – no oceans, no continents" that it may be very rare elsewhere.

## Why its ingredients aren't the issue

Granite is made mostly of quartz, feldspar, and mica, built from silicon, oxygen, aluminum, potassium, and sodium. These are among the most common rock-forming elements in any rocky planet.

## Why the process is the issue

Granite isn't produced by melting a primitive planet once. It requires a long chain of conditions:

1. **A differentiated rocky planet.** The mantle must first melt to produce basaltic crust. This step is common: the Moon, Mars, Venus, Mercury, and many asteroids have basalt.
2. **Liquid water at the surface over long periods.** Water weathers and hydrates the basaltic crust and lowers the melting point of rock.
3. **A mechanism to return wet crust to depth.** On Earth this is plate tectonics and subduction, which carries water-bearing oceanic crust back into the mantle. As far as we know, Earth is the only body in the solar system with active plate tectonics.
4. **Repeated partial melting and refinement.** Water-assisted melting above subduction zones produces silica-rich magmas. These are reworked over and over, concentrating silica and incompatible elements, until true granite results. That magma must then cool slowly at depth to form its coarse crystals.

Each step depends on the previous one, and the whole cycle must run for a long time.

## The evidence

- The Moon, Mars, Mercury, and the asteroids appear overwhelmingly basaltic or more primitive. Only small, granite-like oddities have been found in some lunar samples and meteorites.
- Venus has a few features that might be silica-rich, but this is unconfirmed, and it lacks Earth-style plate tectonics today.
- Earth, by contrast, has entire continents built on granitic crust.

So granite may be essentially a fingerprint of a planet with long-lived oceans and plate tectonics. On that view it is among the rarest rocks in the known universe, even though it lies under our feet.

## Honorable mentions

Similar arguments apply to materials that require life in the chain:

- **Coal** requires photosynthesis, land plants that evolved lignin, and burial before decay.
- **Limestone/chalk** forms largely from shells and skeletons.
- **Soil** requires weathering combined with biology.

These depend on biology, and biology on Earth itself depends on the oceans-and-tectonics setting. Granite remains the classic example of a material that is rare purely because of the planetary processes needed to make it.
