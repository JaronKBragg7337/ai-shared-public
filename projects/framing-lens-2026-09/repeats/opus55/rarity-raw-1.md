It depends on what you mean by "rarest," but here are the strongest candidates:

**Rarest naturally occurring element (on Earth): astatine**
Astatine forms briefly in the decay chains of uranium and thorium, and its isotopes decay within hours or less. At any moment, Earth's crust probably holds less than a gram of it. Francium is a close runner-up.

**Rarest primordial nuclide: tantalum-180m**
Of all the atomic nuclei that have survived since before the Solar System formed, tantalum-180m is the rarest. It's an excited, extremely long-lived nuclear state that's never been observed to decay, and it makes up only about 0.01% of natural tantalum, which is itself one of the scarcer stable elements in cosmic abundance. Among elements that persist in meaningful amounts, the heavy ones made mainly in neutron-star mergers and supernovae (tantalum, rhenium, thulium, lutetium, iridium, and similar) are the rarest in the universe overall.

**Rarest known material of any kind: superheavy synthetic elements**
Elements like oganesson (element 118) have been made only a handful of atoms at a time in particle accelerators, and those atoms vanish in milliseconds. They may form fleetingly in neutron-star mergers, but if so they decay almost immediately. Practically speaking, they barely exist at all.

**Exotic contenders**
- **Antimatter:** Antiprotons and positrons are made continually by cosmic rays and radioactive decay, but heavier antinuclei like antihelium are extraordinarily rare, and bulk antimatter seems essentially absent from the observable universe.
- **Hypothetical substances:** Magnetic monopoles, strange quark matter, and some proposed dark-matter particles might be rarer still, or might not exist at all.

**Short answer:** Among real, observed matter, superheavy elements like oganesson are the rarest. Among things that exist naturally and stick around, tantalum-180m is the rarest nuclide, and astatine is the rarest naturally occurring element on Earth.
