It depends on how you define "rarest," but a few answers stand out:

- **Astatine** (element 85) is probably the rarest naturally occurring element on Earth. The entire crust holds only about 25 grams at any moment, because it is constantly produced and decays from radioactive decay chains of uranium and thorium. Other short-lived elements like francium, technetium, and promethium are similarly scarce.

- **Lithium, beryllium, and boron** are rare in the universe as a whole relative to their neighbors on the periodic table. Big Bang nucleosynthesis and stellar fusion largely skip over them, so they survive mainly from cosmic-ray collisions that break up heavier atoms.

- **Antimatter** is the rarest substance in the observable universe in a broad sense. Matter outnumbers antimatter by a vast margin, and the reason for that imbalance is still an open question in physics. Antimatter exists in small amounts, such as in cosmic rays, radioactive decays, and particle accelerators, but it is almost never found in bulk.

If you mean the rarest *stable, abundant-enough-to-study* material, astatine is the usual answer. If you mean the rarest in the universe as a whole, antimatter is a strong contender.
