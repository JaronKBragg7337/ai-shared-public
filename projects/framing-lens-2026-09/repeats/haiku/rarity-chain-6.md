**Soil** is a strong candidate.

Its ingredients are ordinary: silicate minerals, water, carbon, nitrogen, and other common elements. It also doesn't need an unusual temperature or pressure range. What makes it rare is the chain of conditions that has to hold for a long time:

- **Geology** must first supply bedrock, and tectonic uplift must expose it to weather.
- **Climate** must provide liquid water and cycles of freezing, thawing, or wetting and drying to break rock down.
- **Chemistry** must allow weathering reactions that turn rock into clay and mineral fragments.
- **Biology** must arrive and persist. Microbes, fungi, plants, and animals have to fix carbon and nitrogen, decompose dead matter, and mix organic and mineral material into humus.
- **Time** must be long enough, often thousands of years for a few centimeters of fertile topsoil.
- **Stability** must last. Erosion, glaciation, fire, or flooding can strip soil faster than it forms.

Many planets and moons may have rock, water, and carbon, but few are known to have all these processes running together over long periods. Earth's soil is therefore the product of a rare, interlocking sequence of planetary, chemical, and biological events, not of rare ingredients.

Other candidates, such as coal (which needs plants, rapid burial, and oxygen-poor conditions) or amber, fit the same logic, but soil is the most familiar example.
