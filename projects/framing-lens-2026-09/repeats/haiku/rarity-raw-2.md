It depends on how you measure "rarest," but a few answers stand out:

- **Astatine (At)** is the rarest naturally occurring element on Earth. It's a short-lived decay product of uranium and thorium, and the entire crust holds only a few grams at most at any given time. Francium is similarly scarce.
- **Technetium and promethium** have no stable isotopes, so they've decayed away over cosmic time. Technetium is still made in some aging stars, and it has been detected spectroscopically there, but its natural supply is tiny.
- **Antimatter** is arguably the rarest "material" in the observable universe. Positrons and antiprotons are produced in cosmic rays, lightning, and some nuclear processes, but the universe is overwhelmingly made of ordinary matter, and the reason for that imbalance is an open question in physics.
- **Among stable elements**, the least abundant in the solar system are some rare-earth metals such as thulium and lutetium, along with platinum-group metals like rhodium, iridium, and osmium. Their abundances are tiny compared with hydrogen, helium, oxygen, and carbon.

If you mean the rarest *stable* material, thulium is a common answer. If you mean the rarest thing that exists at all, antimatter is the strongest candidate.
