**Wood** is the best candidate.

Its ingredients are among the most common in the universe: carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, assembled into cellulose and lignin. It also forms at ordinary temperatures and pressures. What makes it rare is the long chain of things that must happen before nature can produce it:

1. A planet stable and habitable for billions of years, with liquid water.
2. The origin of life.
3. The evolution of photosynthesis.
4. Complex cells, then multicellular organisms.
5. Plants colonizing land.
6. The evolution of vascular tissue and lignin, and then of true woody growth, in which a cambium layer adds new rings each year.

Even on Earth, that last step came late. Wood first appeared roughly 400 million years ago, so for about 90% of the planet's history there was none here either.

Compare that with diamond, which we treat as precious. Diamond needs only carbon and pressure, so it likely exists in vast quantities across the cosmos: as nanodiamonds in meteorites and interstellar dust, inside carbon-rich planets, and in crystallized white dwarf stars. Wood, as far as we know, exists on exactly one planet.

Two caveats apply. First, the argument depends on how common life is elsewhere, which we don't know; if complex plant-like life is widespread, wood-like materials might be too. Second, the same logic applies to other biological materials, such as bone, shell, silk, amber and coal (the last two themselves depend on trees). Wood is simply the most familiar and striking example.
