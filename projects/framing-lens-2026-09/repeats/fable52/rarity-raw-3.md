There's no single agreed answer, because it depends on what counts as a "material" and how you measure rarity. The main candidates are:

- **Rarest stable element: tantalum.** Among elements that don't decay away, tantalum is generally cited as the least abundant in the solar system, and probably in the universe at large. Its isotope tantalum-180m is the rarest long-lived nuclide found in nature, making up only about 0.01% of an already scarce element.

- **Rarest naturally occurring element: astatine.** It is so radioactive (its longest-lived isotope has a half-life of about 8 hours) that less than a gram is estimated to exist in the Earth's crust at any moment. It exists only because the decay of uranium and thorium constantly replenishes it. Francium is a close runner-up.

- **Rarest element ever observed: oganesson (element 118).** Only a handful of atoms have ever been made in the lab, and each lasted under a millisecond. Violent events like neutron star mergers may briefly create superheavy elements, but they vanish almost instantly.

- **Exotic possibilities.** Antimatter is extraordinarily rare compared with ordinary matter. Hypothetical materials like strange quark matter may not exist at all.

If I had to pick one, I'd say tantalum-180m for something that persists in nature, or oganesson for something known to have existed at all. But we can only sample a tiny corner of the cosmos directly, so the abundance figures beyond our solar system are inferences from spectroscopy and nucleosynthesis models.
