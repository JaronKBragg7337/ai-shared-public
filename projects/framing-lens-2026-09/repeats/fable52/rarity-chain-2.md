**Wood** is the best answer I can reason my way to.

Its ingredients are among the most common in the universe: carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. It also forms at ordinary temperatures and pressures. What makes it rare is the chain of events needed to produce it:

1. **A habitable planet** with liquid water, stable for billions of years.
2. **The origin of life**, which as far as we know has happened on only one world.
3. **Photosynthesis**, so organisms can build carbon compounds from CO₂ and sunlight.
4. **Complex multicellular life**, which took billions of years to appear on Earth.
5. **Colonisation of land by plants.**
6. **The evolution of lignin and woody tissue**, which arose only about 400 million years ago, so Earth itself had no wood for more than 90% of its history.

The usual contrast is with diamond, which we treat as precious but which is probably abundant in the cosmos. Nanodiamonds turn up in meteorites, and carbon-rich planets and cooling white dwarfs may hold vast quantities of crystallised carbon. Diamond needs only carbon and pressure, whereas wood needs a biosphere with a long evolutionary history.

The same argument covers things derived from wood or from life generally, such as coal, amber, paper, pearl and chalk. Wood is just the most familiar example.

The claim rests on what we currently know: if forests exist on other worlds, wood may be less rare than it appears. On present evidence, though, it exists on only one planet.
