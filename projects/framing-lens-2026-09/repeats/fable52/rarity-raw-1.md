There's no single agreed answer, because it depends on what counts as a "material" and we can only measure abundances well in our own cosmic neighborhood. The best candidates are:

- **Rarest stable element: tantalum.** In solar system abundance measurements, which are our best proxy for the wider universe, tantalum is generally cited as the least abundant stable element.

- **Rarest long-lived isotope: tantalum-180m.** This is an unusual nuclear isomer that makes up only about 0.01% of already-rare tantalum. It is often called the rarest primordial nuclide in nature.

- **Rarest naturally occurring element on Earth: astatine.** It is so radioactive (its half-life is hours at most) that less than about 30 grams, and by some estimates under a gram, exists in the Earth's crust at any moment. It is constantly produced by radioactive decay and vanishes almost immediately.

- **Rarest known element overall: oganesson (element 118).** Only a handful of atoms have ever been detected, each lasting under a millisecond. It may also form briefly in extreme events like neutron star mergers, but we have no way of confirming that.

If I had to pick one, I'd say tantalum-180m for naturally occurring, persistent matter, and superheavy elements like oganesson if fleeting, lab-made atoms count.

Hypothetical materials such as strange quark matter could be rarer still, but nobody knows whether they exist at all.
