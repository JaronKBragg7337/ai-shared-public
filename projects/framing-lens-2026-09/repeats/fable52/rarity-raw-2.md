It depends on what counts as a "material," so there are a few defensible answers:

- **Rarest stable element:** Tantalum is generally cited as the least abundant stable element in the solar system, and probably in the universe at large. Its isotope tantalum-180m is the rarest naturally occurring nuclide known that has survived since before the solar system formed.

- **Rarest naturally occurring element on Earth:** Astatine. It is so radioactive (its longest-lived isotope has a half-life of about 8 hours) that less than a gram is estimated to exist in the entire Earth's crust at any moment. Since it decays almost as soon as it forms, it is presumably rare everywhere else too.

- **Rarest elements ever observed:** Superheavy synthetic elements such as oganesson (element 118). Only a handful of atoms have ever been made, and each lasted less than a millisecond. Some might form briefly in events like neutron star mergers, but we have no evidence of them existing naturally.

- **Rare by comparison with ordinary matter:** Antimatter. The universe is overwhelmingly made of matter, and antimatter exists only fleetingly, in cosmic rays, radioactive decay and high-energy events, before it annihilates.

If I had to pick one, I'd say tantalum-180m for things that persist naturally, and superheavy elements like oganesson for anything known to have existed at all. We can only measure abundances well in our own cosmic neighborhood, though, so any claim about the whole cosmos is an extrapolation.
