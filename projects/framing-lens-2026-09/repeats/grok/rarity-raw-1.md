**Astatine, if you want a named element. Heavier antimatter, if you want the actual cosmic answer.**

“Rarest material” is not a single scientific category. Abundance depends on whether you count atoms that last, atoms that exist only as decay leftovers, or bulk stuff you could in principle hold.

**Among named chemical elements, astatine is the usual winner.** It has no stable isotopes. The longest-lived one that shows up in nature, astatine-210, has an 8.1-hour half-life; the isotopes that appear in uranium and thorium decay chains live minutes or seconds. It is produced, then it is gone. Francium is in the same club. At any instant, the universe contains some of both, but only as a thin, continuously replenished film of decay products sitting on top of much longer-lived heavy nuclei.

That is an Earth-and-textbook answer. It is still a fair one: both elements are real, named, and exist in nature, and their standing inventory is tiny.

**In the cosmos as a whole, heavier antimatter is rarer.** Ordinary baryonic matter is almost entirely hydrogen and helium. Everything heavier is a rounding error from stellar burning, supernovae, and neutron-star mergers. Antimatter is rarer still. The early universe left a slight excess of matter; the rest annihilated. What antimatter exists now is secondary: positrons and antiprotons from cosmic-ray collisions, plus whatever particle accelerators make. Bound antihydrogen is already scarce. Antihelium and heavier antinuclei, if they exist as cosmic-ray particles at all, are among the rarest objects ever claimed in nature. A few candidate events would, if real, be the entire known inventory.

**Rarer still, and briefer, are the heaviest nuclei.** The r-process in neutron-star mergers can assemble superheavy nuclei for a moment. Many of those species live microseconds or less. They are materials only in the sense that they are atoms. Then they decay. At a random time, the universe may contain only a handful of the most extreme of them, or none.

So:

- **Astatine / francium** — rarest familiar elements that actually occur in nature.
- **Antihelium and heavier antinuclei** — rarest bulk-style matter the cosmos still produces.
- **The heaviest, shortest-lived superheavy nuclei** — rarest atoms, if you count things that exist for a flash after a merger.

If the question wants one name, **astatine**. If it wants the thing that is actually scarcest in the universe, **heavy antimatter**.
