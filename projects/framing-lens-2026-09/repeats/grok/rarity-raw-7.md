**Tantalum-180m is the rarest ordinary material that actually persists in the cosmos.** It is a long-lived nuclear form of tantalum, and tantalum is already the scarcest stable element.

What “rarest” means depends on the cutoff:

- **Stable elements.** Tantalum is the least abundant element with a stable isotope in the Sun’s mix, which is a good stand-in for the Milky Way’s chemical inventory. Stellar fusion and later neutron-capture events barely make it. You get on the order of one tantalum atom per trillion hydrogen atoms.
- **Long-lived nuclear species.** Only about 0.012% of that tantalum is the isomer tantalum-180m. It is observationally stable, with a half-life far longer than the age of the universe, and it is the rarest primordial nuclide known. That puts it near one atom per 10^16 hydrogen atoms.
- **Elements with no stable form.** Astatine and francium are made in nature, in uranium and thorium decay chains and in neutron-star mergers, then disappear in hours or minutes. Their standing cosmic stock is far below tantalum’s because nothing accumulates. On Earth the whole crust holds well under a gram of astatine at a time; francium, with a 22-minute half-life for its longest-lived isotope, is scarcer still.
- **A kind of matter, rather than an element.** Antimatter is rarer than any of these as a bulk material. After the Big Bang, matter won. What remains is a trace of positrons and antiprotons in cosmic rays and in radioactive decays. No antimatter stars, planets, or rocks are known.

Lab-made superheavy elements exist only a few atoms at a time and vanish in milliseconds, so they are rarer still, but they are not a cosmic material.
