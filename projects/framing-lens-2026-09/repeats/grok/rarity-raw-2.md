**Astatine is the rarest chemical element that actually occurs in nature.**

Every astatine isotope is radioactive. The longest-lived, astatine-210, lasts about eight hours, and the isotopes that show up in uranium and thorium decay chains last seconds or less. Astatine never accumulates. It is made only as a brief side product of decays that are already scarce, then it disappears. At any moment the whole Earth holds roughly a gram of it, or less. The same steady-state arithmetic makes it vanishingly scarce everywhere else in the cosmos.

Two other readings of “rarest material” point somewhere else:

- **Rarest substance that lasts.** That is the nuclear isomer **tantalum-180m**. It is treated as stable over cosmic time, yet it is only about one atom in 8,000 of tantalum, and tantalum is already among the least abundant stable elements. It is made almost only in rare, violent events such as neutron-star mergers. Order of magnitude: one tantalum-180m atom for every 10¹⁶ hydrogen atoms.
- **Rarest form of matter, if antimatter counts.** Bulk antimatter is rarer than either. The cosmos is matter. Antimatter appears as single particles from cosmic rays and radioactive decay. No antimatter stars, clouds, or rocks are known.

So: astatine for a natural element, tantalum-180m for a lasting primordial material, and antimatter if any form of matter is allowed. The abundance figures above are order-of-magnitude estimates from nuclear data and solar-system abundances; the ranking does not depend on the exact powers of ten.
