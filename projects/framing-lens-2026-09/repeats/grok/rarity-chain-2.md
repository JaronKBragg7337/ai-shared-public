**Coal.**

A lump of ordinary coal is made of cosmically ordinary stuff — mostly carbon, with hydrogen, oxygen, and a little nitrogen and sulfur — and it does not form in some razor-thin corner of temperature and pressure. Burial and mild heating turn peat into coal across a broad range of conditions. What is extreme is the chain that has to happen first, in order, before that chemistry can even start.

Nature has to produce a long-lived rocky world with surface water, then life, then oxygenic photosynthesis, then plants that leave the water and invent wood. Lignin, the polymer that stiffens wood, is unusually hard to break down. Those plants then have to grow in enormous waterlogged forests where dead wood is buried in oxygen-poor peat faster than it can rot, in basins that keep sinking so the peat is preserved rather than eroded. Only after that does ordinary geological cooking turn the peat into coal, and the seam has to survive later tectonics and erosion.

One influential version of this argument adds a historical accident: woody plants may have flourished for a long interval before fungi evolved an efficient way to digest lignin, so Carboniferous swamps laid down peat on a scale later eras never matched. Later work also credits climate and the layout of the continents, but that does not shorten the chain. There is still no abiotic route to coal. Remove land plants, or swamps, or burial, and the material never appears.

Wood and soil also require a biosphere, but they are ongoing products: a world with land plants keeps making them. Coal is what you get only when that biosphere’s output is caught, buried, and held through a particular geological history. Diamonds need pressure, and gold needs scarce atoms. Coal needs a sequence of biological and geological events stacked on top of one another, which is why it can reasonably be argued to be among the rarest materials nature produces.
