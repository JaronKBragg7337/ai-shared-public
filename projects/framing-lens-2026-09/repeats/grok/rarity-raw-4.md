Among ordinary atomic matter, the rarest long-lived nuclear species is **tantalum-180m**.

It is a nuclear isomer: the same nucleus as tantalum-180, held in an excited state that happens to be so long-lived it is treated as stable. Only about 0.012% of natural tantalum is this isomer, and tantalum itself already sits near the bottom of the cosmic abundance curve. Stellar fusion and the usual neutron-capture routes produce it very inefficiently, so its share of the universe’s ordinary matter is tiny.

Two nearby answers depend on what you count:

- **Astatine** is the rarest element present at any moment. Every isotope is short-lived (the longest half-life is hours), so it exists only as a trace continually rebuilt by the decay of heavier nuclei. In Earth’s crust the standing inventory is on the order of grams.
- **Antimatter** is rarer still if any form of matter counts. The observable universe is matter-dominated. Antimatter shows up only briefly in high-energy events such as cosmic rays and then annihilates.

So for a lasting, naturally occurring material, tantalum-180m is the rarest.
