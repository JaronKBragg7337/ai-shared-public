**Crude oil (petroleum).**

It is made from hydrogen and carbon, two of the most abundant elements in the universe, and it forms under ordinary crustal warmth — roughly the range of a hot drink to a kitchen oven — rather than in some exotic pressure–temperature pocket. What is rare is the sequence that has to finish intact:

- Life, usually plankton, has to yield a lot of organic matter.
- That matter has to be buried in an oxygen-poor basin so it becomes kerogen in a source rock instead of being recycled.
- The rock has to be buried long enough, and deeply enough, for the kerogen to crack into liquid hydrocarbons.
- Those liquids have to be expelled and migrate through permeable rock.
- A porous reservoir, an impermeable seal, and a trap all have to be in place.
- The trap has to exist before or during migration, then stay sealed for geologic time: not faulted open, not eroded, not cooked further into gas, and not destroyed by bacteria.

Each step is plain geology and chemistry. Miss any one and nature never delivers a body of crude oil. Earth has run that chain many times. A random rocky world, even one with oceans and life, could easily never complete and preserve it. A barrel of crude oil is therefore a fair candidate for one of the rarest familiar materials we know of.

Coal supports the same kind of argument through a different path: woody land plants, wetlands where plant matter accumulates faster than it decays, burial, and coalification, with an extra evolutionary contingency in the long gap before lignin was efficiently decomposed.
