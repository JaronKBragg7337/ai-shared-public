The rarest lasting chemical substance is the nuclear isomer **tantalum-180m**.

Tantalum is already one of the least abundant stable elements. Stars make it only in tiny amounts, off the main peaks of the processes that build heavy nuclei. Of those atoms, only about one in eight thousand is tantalum-180m, an excited nuclear state that is effectively stable over cosmic time. No other primordial nucleus is scarcer in the solar system, which is our best local sample of ordinary cosmic matter.

Two broader readings of “material” point to things even scarcer:

- **Astatine** (and, similarly, **francium**) if short-lived elements count. Astatine’s longest-lived isotope lasts about eight hours, so it never builds up. It exists only as a brief step in decay chains. The whole of Earth’s crust holds on the order of grams at any moment.
- **Antimatter** if every form of matter counts. The early universe made matter and antimatter in nearly equal amounts, and they annihilated. What remains is a small excess of matter. No star or galaxy of antimatter has been seen. The antimatter we actually measure is a trace of antiparticles in cosmic rays.
