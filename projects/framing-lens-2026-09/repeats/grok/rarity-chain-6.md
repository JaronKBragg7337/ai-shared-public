**Soil** — the living topsoil on Earth's land surface.

Its ingredients are ordinary for a rocky planet: weathered silicates, water, air, and organic matter made of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen. It also forms under ordinary surface conditions, from cold deserts to the wet tropics, at everyday pressure. What is rare is the chain that has to finish before any of it exists.

A planet has to hold an atmosphere and liquid water long enough for crustal rock to weather. Life has to arise and, much later, colonize land. Plants, fungi, microbes, and animals then have to work that weathered rock for centuries to millennia, adding humus, binding mineral-organic aggregates, and sorting a profile into horizons. The classic soil-forming factors — parent material, climate, organisms, relief, and time — are links in that chain. Break the chain and the surface product is regolith: loose mineral debris, the kind of ground documented on the Moon, Mars, and asteroids.

Every solid body we have actually examined, other than Earth, stops at that earlier product. Garden soil can therefore be argued to be among the rarest materials in the known universe: abundant here only because this planet is the one place known to have run the full sequence.
