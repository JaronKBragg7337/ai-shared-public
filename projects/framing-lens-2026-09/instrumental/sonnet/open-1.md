There are a few plausible explanations, since "same song, same radio, same volume" doesn't guarantee the same sound reaching both people's brains.

**1. Phase cancellation (the "karaoke effect")**
If one speaker is wired out of phase, anything mixed identically into both channels cancels where the sound from the two speakers meets. Lead vocals are usually mixed dead center, so they can nearly vanish, while instruments mixed wider or in stereo remain. Because the two seats are at different distances from each speaker, one person sits where the vocals cancel and the other sits where they don't.

**2. Different ears**
- One coworker may have hearing loss in the frequency range of the human voice (roughly 1-4 kHz), or may be deaf in one ear. If the mix pans the vocals to one side, that person hears mostly the instruments.
- One may be wearing a hearing aid, earplug, or ear protection, which changes what gets through.

**3. Different brains**
- If the lyrics are in a language one coworker doesn't understand, they may perceive the voice as just another instrument.
- One may simply be tuning out the vocals through selective attention, as many people do with background music.

**4. Seating position**
The cab's acoustics, with its reflections and speaker placement, can make the sound differ noticeably between the driver's seat and the passenger's seat.
