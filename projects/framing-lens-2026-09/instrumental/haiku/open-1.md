The sound reaching both ears is the same. The difference is in what each brain does with it. Hearing is the physical detection of sound, but understanding that a stream of sound contains words takes processing that depends on the listener. A few things could account for it:

- **Language.** If the lyrics are in a language one person doesn't know, the words still arrive, but they register as sung syllables inside the melody. Speech in an unfamiliar language is hard to segment into words, so it sounds like part of the music. This is a common experience with foreign-language songs.
- **Familiarity.** Someone who knows the song has its words stored, so the brain automatically matches the sounds to them. A stranger to the song hears only a melody, because nothing tells the brain to extract words from the sound.
- **Attention.** Lyrics are easy to miss when you're thinking, talking, or focused on driving. Attention acts as a filter (the "cocktail party effect"), and the same sound can be foreground for one listener and background for the other.
- **Hearing differences.** Lyrics are carried largely by consonants and higher frequencies, which are the first to degrade with age or noise damage. One person might have slightly worse high-frequency hearing, so the vocals blur into the instruments.
- **Position and noise.** Seat location relative to the speakers, road noise, or the other person's conversation can mask consonants that the other person catches.
- **Musical training.** Some listeners parse sung text more easily, and others attend mainly to pitch, rhythm, and harmony.

So the question treats the song as the thing that has lyrics. The lyrics actually exist only once a listener decodes them, and that decoding depends on language, memory, attention, and hearing.
