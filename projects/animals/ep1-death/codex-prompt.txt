[s1] When an elephant stands over a body for hours, is she mourning? When crows gather around a fallen crow, is it a funeral? This is Animals, episode one: do animals understand death? On every screen we will keep two things apart: what scientists actually observed, and what people read into it.
[s2] First, what would understanding death even mean? In studies of how children learn about death, researchers use parts like these: it is permanent, the body stops working, and it happens to everything alive. Separately, there is the question of feeling: do animals grieve? Watching behavior can show what an animal does near the dead. On its own, it cannot show what the animal knows or feels.
[s3] Elephants first. In 2006, researchers in Kenya described what happened when an old matriarch named Eleanor collapsed. Another matriarch, Grace, came to her, touched her with her tusks, and tried to lift her. Eleanor died the next day. In the following days, elephants from several families visited her body. In a separate 2006 experiment in Amboseli National Park, elephants were shown elephant skulls, ivory, other animals' skulls, and a log. They spent more time with the elephant skulls and the ivory.
[s4] So what does it mean? One reading: elephants recognize a lost individual, and they grieve. Another reading: elephants are drawn to the smell and shape of their own species' remains, which is not the same as knowing that a death happened. The Amboseli result shows interest in their own kind's remains. It does not show that elephants grasp what death is. Stories of elephants covering bodies with branches exist, but they are anecdotes.
[s5] Primates. In 2010, researchers reported on Pansy, an elderly chimpanzee who died in a Scottish safari park. Her group inspected and groomed her body, some stayed close overnight, and for days afterward the group avoided the place where she died. In Guinea, at Bossou, scientists documented chimpanzee mothers carrying their dead infants for weeks. And in Botswana, a 2006 study of baboons found that females who lost a close relative to a predator had higher stress hormones afterward, and then groomed a wider circle of partners.
[s6] Here the debate is sharp. A 2019 critical review by Goncalves and Carvalho looked across the primate literature. They found many reports, but mostly single events described after the fact, and little to show that animals know the dead are gone for good. A mother who carries an infant for weeks may be deeply attached, or she may not read the body as dead. Others, such as anthropologist Barbara King, argue that grief is a reasonable reading. Both sides agree on the data: behavior changes after a death. They differ on what it means.
[s7] Birds. In 2012, a team in California reported that western scrub-jays gather around dead jays, calling loudly, and many stop foraging for a while. They described these gatherings as funerals, but the authors suspected the real purpose was learning about danger. In 2015, researchers in Washington State found that wild crows gather around dead crows and scold. Afterward, the crows treated the place, and nearby people, with more caution. That points to learning about risk. Whether there is any mourning on top of that is not known.
[s8] Dolphins and their relatives. A 2016 review by Reggente and colleagues collected cases of dolphins and whales supporting or carrying dead companions, often calves. In 2018, an orca named Tahlequah, in the Pacific Northwest, carried her dead newborn calf for seventeen days, across roughly a thousand miles. Orcas are the largest members of the dolphin family. Observed: she carried the calf and did not leave it. Interpretation: grief, or an instinct to keep a calf at the surface, or both. Nobody has tested those apart.
[s9] So where does this leave the question? Well supported: many species notice and react to dead companions. They touch, guard, carry, or avoid them. Some animals show stress changes after a loss. Not established: that any animal knows death is permanent, that it comes to everyone, or that what they feel is grief as people know it. The behavior is real. The meaning is open.
[s10] Two traps. One is reading human feelings into every report. We notice the striking cases and share the videos, while animals that ignore a body rarely make the news. The other has a name from the primatologist Frans de Waal: anthropodenial, refusing to see anything in animals that people also have. Careful scientists try to avoid both.
[s11] What would settle it? Comparing how animals respond to the dead, the dying, the sleeping, and the sedated, recorded the same way each time. Long-term field records that include the times nothing happens. Hormone measures across many deaths. And tests that separate smell and curiosity from attachment. Researchers have proposed approaches like these, but they are hard, and nobody should harm an animal to find out.
[s12] For now, the honest answer is: not established. Animals clearly notice death. Whether they understand it the way a person does remains an open question, and the evidence is still being gathered.
