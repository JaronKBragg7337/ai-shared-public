## Problems

- **WRONG — [s3]** Quote: “Grace came to her, touched her with her tusks, and tried to lift her.” Correction: The paper says Grace first touched Eleanor with her trunk and foot, then lifted her with her tusks and continued nudging her with them. [Douglas-Hamilton et al. (2006)](https://www.savetheelephants.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/2006_D-H_etal_DeathOfMatriarch_16Pages.pdf)

- **UNSURE — [s3]** Quote: “In the following days, elephants from several families visited her body.” Correction: Elephants from other families did approach and investigate the body, but the paper’s account combines direct observations with movements inferred from GPS tracking. The script should distinguish witnessed visits from inferred proximity. [Douglas-Hamilton et al. (2006)](https://www.savetheelephants.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/2006_D-H_etal_DeathOfMatriarch_16Pages.pdf)

- **UNSURE — [s4]** Quote: “elephants are drawn to the smell and shape of their own species’ remains” Correction: The Amboseli experiment supports greater interest in elephant ivory and skulls than in the comparison objects. It does not identify smell or shape as the cause; researchers washed the objects to control for scent differences and did not test those sensory explanations separately. [McComb, Baker & Moss (2006)](https://r.jordan.im/download/mammals/mccomb2006.pdf)

- **UNSURE — [s4]** Quote: “Stories of elephants covering bodies with branches exist, but they are anecdotes.” Correction: This needs a specific, traceable source. As written, it is too vague to check which reports are meant or whether they are genuinely anecdotal rather than documented observations. The study cited in the script does not establish this claim. [McComb, Baker & Moss (2006)](https://r.jordan.im/download/mammals/mccomb2006.pdf)

- **UNSURE — [s6]** Quote: “They found many reports, but mostly single events described after the fact, and little to show that animals know the dead are gone for good.” Correction: The review does describe a sparse literature with many reports of individual events, and it distinguishes evidence for recognizing a body as dead from evidence for a broader concept of death. But the summary omits the authors’ own conclusion: they argue that nonhuman primates are capable of *implicit awareness of death*. [Gonçalves & Carvalho (2019)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/brv.12512)

- **UNSURE — [s6]** Quote: “Both sides agree on the data: behavior changes after a death. They differ on what it means.” Correction: This makes the debate sound like two clearly defined camps with a shared, settled interpretation of the evidence. King argues that grief is a reasonable reading, while Gonçalves and Carvalho advance a positive claim about implicit death awareness; the review also proposes possible functions for death-related behavior. Say that researchers disagree about what the behavior supports, and name the specific claims being compared. [Gonçalves & Carvalho (2019)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/brv.12512), [King, *How Animals Grieve*](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/H/bo13636327.html)

- **UNSURE — [s7]** Quote: “western scrub-jays gather around dead jays, calling loudly, and many stop foraging for a while.” Correction: The study used experimental presentations of dead-jay skins at feeders. Jays formed calling aggregations and reduced foraging during presentations; the decrease was still detectable 24 hours later and returned to baseline by 48 hours. Identify this as an experimental result, rather than leaving it to sound like an observation of spontaneous gatherings around naturally dead birds. [Iglesias, McElreath & Patricelli (2012)](https://patricellilab.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/147/2014/12/Iglesias-et-al-Scrub-jay-funerals-AnimBehav-2012.pdf)

- **WRONG — [s7]** Quote: “the authors suspected the real purpose was learning about danger.” Correction: The researchers tested whether dead conspecifics function as cues of risk and reported results supporting that interpretation. “Real purpose” overstates what the experiment establishes about function or intent; describe it as the study’s supported risk-information explanation. [Iglesias, McElreath & Patricelli (2012)](https://patricellilab.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/147/2014/12/Iglesias-et-al-Scrub-jay-funerals-AnimBehav-2012.pdf)

- **UNSURE — [s7]** Quote: “wild crows gather around dead crows and scold. Afterward, the crows treated the place, and nearby people, with more caution.” Correction: Swift and Marzluff experimentally presented dead crows and other danger cues. The crows mobbed and scolded, became more cautious about approaching food in the associated area, and learned to scold people seen near those cues. Clarify that the reported results came from staged presentations and learned associations. [Swift & Marzluff (2015)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003347215003188)

- **UNSURE — [s8]** Quote: “Observed: she carried the calf and did not leave it.” Correction: Reports support that Tahlequah carried the calf over 17 days, but the absolute “did not leave it” is stronger than the reported observation; other pod members were also reported taking turns carrying the body. A more precise version is: “She carried the calf for 17 days, with pod members also involved.” [Center for Whale Research reporting, via Phys.org (2018)](https://phys.org/news/2018-08-wont-dead-orca-calf-mother.html)

- **UNSURE — [s8]** Quote: “Nobody has tested those apart.” Correction: The script does not identify the scope of “those.” The cited Reggente review discusses multiple possible explanations for cetacean behavior toward dead young, but it does not establish Tahlequah’s motivation. Safer wording: “The observations of Tahlequah do not tell us which explanation motivated her behavior.” [Reggente et al. (2016)](https://www.cascadiaresearch.org/files/Reggente_et_al_2016_JMAMM.pdf)

- **UNSURE — [s9, s12]** Quote: “Not established: that any animal knows death is permanent” / “Animals clearly notice death.” Correction: “Any animal” and “animals” make broad claims about all species. Evidence documents responses in particular species; primate reviews also distinguish evidence for recognizing a body as dead from evidence for understanding death’s permanence or universality. Use “Some species clearly react to dead companions” and specify what has not been established. [Gonçalves & Carvalho (2019)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/brv.12512)

- **UNSURE — [s10]** Quote: “We notice the striking cases and share the videos, while animals that ignore a body rarely make the news.” Correction: This is a plausible media-selection-bias observation, but the script gives no source or data to support it. Either cite evidence about reporting bias or present it explicitly as a caution, not an established research finding.

## Fine as written

- **[s1–s2]** The opening frames the question without claiming that observed behavior proves mourning. The three components of a human death concept—irreversibility, nonfunctionality, and universality—are standard components in developmental research. [Speece & Brent (1984)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6510050/). The point that behavior alone cannot establish an animal’s knowledge or feelings is appropriately cautious.
- **[s3]** Eleanor’s identity, the Samburu setting, the 2006 publication, and the separate Amboseli study are accurate. The Amboseli study found greater interest in elephant ivory and skulls than in the comparison objects; it also found no clear preference for a matriarch’s own skull over other matriarch skulls. [Douglas-Hamilton et al. (2006)](https://www.savetheelephants.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/2006_D-H_etal_DeathOfMatriarch_16Pages.pdf), [McComb, Baker & Moss (2006)](https://r.jordan.im/download/mammals/mccomb2006.pdf)
- **[s5]** Pansy’s case, the Bossou dead-infant carrying reports, and the broad summary of the chacma baboon findings are consistent with the cited literature. The Pansy paper is by Anderson, Gillies and Lock; the Bossou 2010 report is by Biro and colleagues. [Anderson, Gillies & Lock (2010)](https://dspace.stir.ac.uk/handle/1893/2834), [Biro et al. (2010)](https://www.greencorridor.info/data/Biro2010.pdf), [Engh et al. (2006)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16608690/)
- **[s6]** Barbara J. King is accurately presented as an advocate for taking animal grief seriously. [King, *How Animals Grieve*](https://barbarajking.com/books/how-animals-grieve/)
- **[s8]** Reggente et al. (2016) is a relevant source on mammal responses to dead conspecifics. Tahlequah’s 17-day carry and the estimate of roughly 1,000 miles are supported by 2018 reporting based on the Center for Whale Research; orcas are the largest dolphins. [Reggente et al. (2016)](https://www.cascadiaresearch.org/files/Reggente_et_al_2016_JMAMM.pdf), [2018 report](https://www.spokesman.com/stories/2018/aug/11/after-17-days-and-1000-miles-mother-orca-tahlequah/)
- **[s10]** De Waal’s 1999 attribution of *anthropodenial* is correct. [de Waal (1999)](https://philpapers.org/archive/VIITEA.pdf)
- **[s11–s13]** The proposed research directions are presented as proposals, and the ending preserves uncertainty about animal understanding. The “made with AI” and source-list statements are production claims, not claims established by these studies.
