[s1] What if gravity isn't the full story? Stars in galaxies move faster than the matter we can see should allow. Either there is unseen mass, called dark matter, or gravity behaves differently than we thought. That the mismatch exists is well established. Which answer is right is not. This is Open Questions, episode four. Every study on screen is published, and its source is on screen too.
[s2] First, the mismatch. In nineteen thirty-three, Fritz Zwicky found that galaxies in the Coma Cluster move faster than their visible mass can explain. Starting in nineteen seventy, Vera Rubin and Kent Ford measured how fast stars and gas orbit in spiral galaxies. Far from the center, speeds stay roughly flat, instead of falling as the visible mass predicts under Newtonian gravity.
[s3] The first explanation is dark matter: extra mass that gives off no light, forming a halo around each galaxy. Its gravity would keep outer stars moving fast. Dark matter is not one observation. It is a model, called lambda C D M, that has to fit many observations at once. But no dark matter particle has been confirmed. Underground experiments such as Lux Zeppelin have searched for years and reported no confirmed detection.
[s4] The second explanation is to change gravity. In nineteen eighty-three, Mordehai Milgrom proposed Mond, modified Newtonian dynamics. Below a very small acceleration, gravity would be stronger than Newton's law says. Using one acceleration constant and the visible matter, Mond reproduces the rotation of many galaxies, including a tight link between visible mass and orbital speed reported by Stacy McGaugh and colleagues in twenty sixteen. Dark matter models can also reproduce that link, but researchers disagree on how naturally.
[s5] Mond began as a rule, not a full theory of gravity. Later versions tried to be one: Jacob Bekenstein's in two thousand four, and a relativistic theory by Constantinos Skordis and Tom Zlosnik in twenty twenty-one. But Mond has trouble with galaxy clusters, which still seem to need extra mass even under Mond.
[s6] The best-known test is the Bullet Cluster, two galaxy clusters that collided. Hot gas, which holds most of the ordinary matter, was slowed by the collision and glows in X-rays. In two thousand six, Douglas Clowe and colleagues used gravitational lensing to map the mass, and found most of it apart from the gas, near the galaxies. That is what dark matter predicts, and it is hard for gravity-only changes to explain. Mond supporters note that clusters need some unseen mass even in Mond, and researchers disagree on what that implies.
[s7] The cosmic microwave background, the oldest light we can see, has a pattern of hot and cold spots. In twenty eighteen, the Planck team reported that standard cosmology with dark matter fits that pattern very precisely, with about five times more dark matter than ordinary matter. Gravity-only alternatives have found it hard to match, though the twenty twenty-one relativistic theory reproduces much of it. Dark energy is a separate puzzle, not covered here.
[s8] Now a newer test: wide binaries. These are pairs of stars thousands of times farther apart than the Earth and the Sun, orbiting slowly. Their pull on each other is so weak that Mond predicts they should orbit somewhat faster than Newtonian gravity says. The Gaia space telescope has measured many such pairs.
[s9] In twenty twenty-three, Kyu-Hyun Chae reported that Gaia wide binaries do move faster, matching Mond. In twenty twenty-four, Indranil Banik and colleagues analyzed Gaia pairs too, and found no such effect, favoring Newtonian gravity. Why the difference? Researchers point to how pairs are selected, to hidden third stars that can fake extra speed, and to how orbits are modeled. A late twenty twenty-five paper still describes the results as conflicting. This is open.
[s10] What would settle it? Detecting a dark matter particle in an experiment would be strong evidence for dark matter, and ever more sensitive null results narrow the options. Gaia's next data release, planned for December twenty twenty-six, may allow cleaner wide-binary samples. Radial-velocity measurements of individual pairs can check for hidden companions. And more collisions like the Bullet Cluster test where the mass sits.
[s11] So what does this show? Established: visible matter alone cannot explain these motions under standard gravity, and the dark matter model fits many observations, including the microwave background and cluster collisions. A Mond-style rule also matches many galaxy rotation curves. Not established: what dark matter is, whether any modified gravity can fit clusters and the microwave background, and whether wide binaries depart from Newtonian gravity. Scientists disagree on each.
[s12] This does not settle what gravity does, or what the universe contains. The mismatch is real. The answer could be new matter, new gravity, or something not yet proposed. Every idea has to face the same observations.
