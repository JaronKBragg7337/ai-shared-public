[s1] Did humanity's birthplace have something special? The oldest known fossils of the human line, and of our own species, come from Africa. That part is well established. The open question is why. This is Open Questions, episode three. Every study on screen is published, and its source is on screen too.
[s2] First, what the fossils show. At Laetoli, in Tanzania, footprints about three point seven million years old were pressed into volcanic ash. Most researchers attribute them to Australopithecus afarensis, the species of the Ethiopian skeleton called Lucy. At Olduvai Gorge, also in Tanzania, Mary Leakey found a skull of an early human relative in nineteen fifty-nine, later dated to about one point eight million years. Early members of our own species appear at Jebel Irhoud in Morocco, about three hundred thousand years ago, and at Omo Kibish in Ethiopia, at least about two hundred thirty thousand.
[s3] Genetics points the same way. People in Africa carry more genetic variation than people anywhere else, and variation elsewhere is largely a subset of it. In two thousand seventeen, Carina Schlebusch and colleagues, in Science, estimated that the deepest split among living human populations began roughly three hundred fifty to two hundred sixty thousand years ago. An African origin for our species is well supported. Why there, and where in Africa, is not.
[s4] So there are two broad kinds of explanation. One says the place mattered: Africa, especially the Rift Valley region, had conditions that pushed our ancestors to adapt. The other says the record is skewed: fossils survive and get found there, so the true map may be wider. They are not exclusive, and researchers weigh them differently.
[s5] First, the place mattered. The Rift is a chain of valleys where the crust is pulling apart, putting lakes, highlands, volcanoes and grassland close together. In nineteen ninety-eight, Richard Potts proposed that unstable environments like this favored species able to adapt rather than specialize. He called it variability selection. In twenty eighteen, Potts and colleagues, in Science, reported that at Olorgesailie, in Kenya, shifting climate and tectonic activity coincided with animal turnover and a new stone toolkit. That is a correlation at one site. Whether it explains our origin is still a hypothesis.
[s6] Second, the record is skewed. A fossil needs fast burial. Rift basins collect sediment. Volcanoes drop ash that seals sites and can be dated with potassium-argon and argon-argon methods. Faulting and erosion then expose old layers, as the river did at Olduvai. In humid forests, acidic soil and scavengers destroy bones. So the Rift can be an unusually good archive without being the only place early humans lived.
[s7] Bias has happened before. In nineteen twenty-five, Raymond Dart described the Taung Child from South Africa, but experts expecting an Asian or European origin doubted it for years. Until two thousand five, no fossil chimpanzee had been found at all. Then Sally McBrearty and Nina Jablonski reported teeth about half a million years old from Kenya's Rift Valley. And in twenty ten, the Denisovans were first identified from DNA in a Siberian finger bone.
[s8] A third view widens the map. Jebel Irhoud in Morocco lies thousands of kilometers from Omo in Ethiopia. In twenty eighteen, Eleanor Scerri and colleagues, in Trends in Ecology and Evolution, argued that our species may have emerged from many partly connected populations across Africa, rather than one cradle. That is an interpretation, not a settled result.
[s9] Now a different question: survival. About seventy-four thousand years ago, the Toba volcano in Sumatra erupted, one of the largest eruptions of the last two million years. In nineteen ninety-eight, Stanley Ambrose proposed that its volcanic winter cut human numbers to a small group of survivors. This is called the Toba catastrophe hypothesis.
[s10] Several studies pushed back. In two thousand seven, Michael Petraglia and colleagues, in Science, reported stone tools in India both below and above the Toba ash, with no clear break. In twenty thirteen, Christine Lane and colleagues found Toba ash in Lake Malawi sediment with no sign of a volcanic winter in East Africa. In twenty eighteen, Eugene Smith and colleagues, in Nature, reported people living on the South African coast through the eruption. A few sites cannot show how everyone fared, so this stays contested.
[s11] What would narrow it down? Searching more in places that preserve poorly or are little explored, such as West and Central Africa. Dating more sites precisely. Recovering ancient proteins, which can outlast DNA in warm climates. And comparing more genomes, ancient and living, to test whether our species began in one region or in a network of populations.
[s12] So what does this show? Established: the oldest known fossils of our species are in Africa, genetics agrees our species began there, and the Rift preserves and dates fossils unusually well. Not established: whether Africa's conditions caused our origin or only revealed it, whether our species began in one region or across a network, and whether Toba nearly ended us. Scientists disagree on each.
[s13] This does not settle whether Africa was special. It shows that a map of fossils is partly a map of preservation and of searching. Scientists still argue about how much of the pattern is real, and how much is the record.
