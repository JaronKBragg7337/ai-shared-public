# Science ep7, Part 2: The sky in the ice

Read-through for listening. About 1194 words, roughly 7 minutes 25 seconds at the ep6 voice pace. Follows Part 1, which ends: "The ice doesn't only keep life. It keeps the sky." Revised after two Codex fact checks (FACTCHECK-PART2-codex.md and FACTCHECK2-PART2-codex.md, 2026-11-02).

## 01. The sky of its time

The ice doesn't only keep life. It keeps the sky. The bubbles in an Antarctic ice core hold a little air from long ago. Nobody made it. Nobody chose to save it. It is just the sky of its time, air from a span of years mixed together in the snow, then sealed away in the dark. This is the sky in the ice.

## 02. Snow that breathes

It starts as snow, and fresh snow is mostly air. As more snow falls on top, the old snow packs into firn: a crunchy, porous layer full of open channels. The top tens of meters are still open to the sky. Air moves down through it, like water soaking into sand.

## 03. The seal closes

Deeper down, the weight keeps squeezing. Usually somewhere between fifty and one hundred twenty meters down, the channels pinch shut, and the air is locked into bubbles. It is not one exact line: at one West Antarctic site, there is a lock-in zone about ten meters thick on the way down to the final seal. At Summit in Greenland it is about seventy meters down. At the colder South Pole, about one hundred twenty.

## 04. The air is younger than its ice

Here is the surprise. Because air keeps flowing down through the open snow, the sealed air is always younger than the ice around it. At Law Dome, on the snowy coast of East Antarctica, the gap is about thirty years. On the dry high plateau at Dome C, it is about two thousand years, and in the last ice age it was bigger. Scientists work it out with models, and still debate the details.

## 05. No bubbles at all

Go deeper still, and the bubbles disappear. The pressure pushes the air right into the ice crystals, each gas molecule held in a tiny cage of water molecules. Scientists call it air hydrate. At Dome Fuji in Antarctica, it first shows up around six hundred meters down, and below about eleven hundred meters there are no bubbles at all. The air is still there. You just can't see it.

## 06. Cores that crack and pop

That makes ice cores tricky. In the middle depths, bubbles in a fresh core hold air at about fifty times normal pressure. A small knock can make them burst and the ice crack. Drillers call it the brittle zone. Deeper ice gets less brittle, but once it reaches the surface it can relax, and bubbles or tiny cracks can appear again. Teams let brittle cores rest, and handle them like eggs.

## 07. What ancient air is made of

So what is ancient air made of? Mostly the same as yours: nitrogen and oxygen. Over eight hundred thousand years, oxygen has dropped by only about zero point seven percent of what it was. The big difference is carbon dioxide. Before the industrial era, over those eight hundred thousand years, it ranged from about one hundred seventy-two to three hundred parts per million. In twenty twenty-six, at Mauna Loa in Hawaii, the monthly average was about four hundred thirty-one in June and four hundred twenty-six in September. Roughly one and a half times the old high.

## 08. Methane

Methane tells the same story, louder. Over the six hundred fifty thousand years before the industrial era, it moved between about three hundred fifty parts per billion in ice ages and eight hundred in warm times. In June twenty twenty-six, the global average was about one thousand nine hundred thirty-five: about two point four times the old high.

## 09. Reading air with a laser

How do you get the air out? Scientists crush the ice in a cold vacuum, melt it, or turn it straight to vapor. Melting can make extra carbon dioxide, so it is used for gases such as methane instead. A newer way uses lasers. One turns ten to fifteen grams of ice, about a few sugar cubes, into vapor. Another reads carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide in the one or two milliliters of air that come out.

## 10. What the oldest air did and didn't say

And the six-million-year-old air from Part One? Its argon gave an age, and the ice gave a temperature: Antarctica has cooled about twelve degrees Celsius since then. As of our research, no carbon dioxide number from that air has been published. An earlier Allan Hills study read air from about one to two million years ago: about two hundred four to two hundred eighty-nine parts per million. Its oldest samples read over sixteen hundred. But microbes near the bedrock had added carbon dioxide, so those readings don't show the ancient sky.

## 11. Could you breathe it?

Could you breathe it? It is ordinary nitrogen and oxygen, with less carbon dioxide than outdoor air today. Glacier ice pops and pings as it melts, because its bubbles are under pressure. In Alaska, that crackle is reportedly called bergy seltzer. One company sells Greenland iceberg ice to bars as over one hundred thousand years old. That age is its own claim. And a co-founder said the ice has no bubbles. If so, it can't make that fizz.

## 12. What modern air carries

Ancient ice air was sealed before modern traffic and factory pollution, though nature always had some smoke, dust, pollen and ozone. Today, the United States Environmental Protection Agency names ground-level ozone, a part of smog, and particle pollution as main outdoor asthma triggers. No health benefit from breathing ancient air has been established. And the ice isn't pure: it holds dust, ash and sea salt from its own time, which is exactly what scientists read.

## 13. A library of bottled sky

People bottle the sky on purpose too. At Cape Grim in Tasmania, wind arrives across thousands of kilometers of ocean: some of the cleanest air on Earth. Since April twenty-sixth, nineteen seventy-eight, scientists have filled four to six cylinders a year. The first were repurposed World War Two containers that had held breathing oxygen on American bombers. About one hundred seventy cylinders, by the agency's own count, sit in storage near Melbourne.

## 14. Why keep old air

Why keep old air? Because the future asks new questions. In twenty fourteen, scientists tested Cape Grim's bottles, with air pumped out of Greenland snow, and found four ozone-destroying gases nobody had known were in the air. Using the old bottles, they traced one back to nineteen seventy-eight. Separately, an old scuba tank, filled before the archive began, stretched the archive further back.

## 15. Today's air, sealing later than you'd think

And today's air? It is soaking into the snow now, and it seals later than you'd think. At snowy Law Dome, air at the sealing depth averages about nine years old. So by our own arithmetic, if conditions stay similar, some twenty twenty-six air could be sealed into bubbles around the mid twenty-thirties, mixed with air from other years, in ice from snow that fell decades earlier. This year's snow will trap some future year's air. Either way, our sky is being written into the ice.

## 16. Your own air time capsule

You can bottle your own sky. Fill a jar outside, away from cars, and label the date and place. But seals can leak. Even NOAA's lab flasks slowly lose a little carbon dioxide, and nobody knows how fast a kitchen jar leaks. So the jar is the symbol. What lasts is what you write down: a real infrared sensor's carbon dioxide reading, that day's air quality, and the latest monthly Mauna Loa number. Someday, someone will read the sky of your day. The ice has been doing that for millions of years. Heartbeat Observatory.
