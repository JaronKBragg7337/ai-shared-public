# Ep 7 research: Antarctica's ice, old DNA, and what happens when it melts

Researched 2026-11-02 for the Heartbeat Observatory Science channel. Science only. Every claim has a source number; the
list is at the bottom. **[GK]** = standard textbook science, no single source needed. "Not established" = the evidence is thin.

**Watch-outs for the script (read first):**
- **The 1-million-year seafloor DNA paper came out in October 2022, not 2023** (Armbrecht et al., Nature Communications). [5]
- **No one has ever revived anything from million-year-old Antarctic ice and shown it is truly that old.** The "8-million-year-old
  bacteria" (2007) grew only weakly, and scientists still question whether they were that old or were contamination. [9]
- **The famous "zombie viruses" came from Siberian permafrost (frozen ground), not Antarctic ice, and they infect amoebae, not people.** [10][11]
- **Earth's gravity has not changed in any meaningful way over these timescales.** Say it gently. [GK, see section 6]

---

## 1. The question, in one line
DNA is locked in Antarctic ice and seafloor mud. If the ice melts, can that DNA, or living cells, get into today's world, and
would that old life stay the same or turn into something new?

**Short answer:** Most old DNA is broken into pieces and dead. A few microbes can survive frozen for thousands of years, maybe
much longer. If any came out, today's ecosystem would mostly outcompete them; the small amount that mixes in would do so the
normal way life always has: genes swapping between microbes, then selection over many generations.

## 2. What old ice and old DNA actually exists down there
*Summary: Antarctica holds ice up to about 6 million years old, seafloor DNA about 1 million years old, and living microbes
in lakes buried under the ice. The oldest stuff is mostly deep and locked away.*

**The oldest ice**
- **Allan Hills (East Antarctica), 6 million years old.** Announced 28 Oct 2025 in PNAS by the COLDEX team (NSF). It is the
  oldest ice ever directly dated, more than double the old record (~2.7 million years). Dated by measuring argon in the air
  bubbles. The pieces are not a continuous timeline; they are scattered older chunks. More drilling is planned 2026–2031. [1][2]
  - Why old ice is near the surface there: Allan Hills is a "blue ice area" where wind strips the snow and old ice gets pushed up
    from below. [GK; see 1]
- **Beyond EPICA (Little Dome C), at least 1.2 million years, one continuous record.** EU project, 12 institutions, 10 countries.
  The drill reached bedrock at ~2,800 m in January 2025. The last core samples arrived in Europe in April 2026; analysis is going
  on in 14 European labs. The goal had been 1.5 million years; "at least 1.2 million" is the confirmed figure. [3][4]
- The older EPICA Dome C core gave 800,000 years of continuous air samples (that is where the famous CO2 record comes from). [16]

**Old DNA in mud (sedimentary ancient DNA, "sedaDNA")**
- **Scotia Sea, about 1 million years old.** Sediment cores drilled up to 178 m below the seafloor in 2019 (IODP Expedition 382).
  Mostly DNA from diatoms (tiny glass-shelled algae at the bottom of the food chain). "By far the oldest authenticated marine
  sedaDNA." Cold, low oxygen and no sunlight kept it. Diatoms were abundant in warm periods; there was a big shift ~540,000
  years ago tied to natural warming. Published Nature Communications, 5 Oct 2022. [5]
- For comparison, the oldest DNA from anywhere: **Kap København, Greenland, ~2 million years** (Kjær et al., Nature 2022), from
  sediment, showing a forest with mastodons, reindeer, hares and geese. DNA survived by sticking to mineral grains. [6]

**Life in the ice and in lakes under the ice**
- Antarctica has hundreds of lakes of liquid water under the ice sheet (kept liquid by pressure and the Earth's heat). [GK]
- **Lake Vostok** (under ~3.7 km of ice): bacteria were grown in the lab from refrozen lake ice at 3,593 m (Christner et al. 2001). [12]
  A 2013 study reported thousands of DNA traces, but contamination from the kerosene drilling fluid is a serious, unresolved
  problem; one Russian analysis found fewer than 10 cells per mL, several of them likely drilling contaminants. **What actually
  lives in the lake itself: not established.** [12][13]
- **Lake Whillans** (under ~800 m of ice, drilled cleanly in Jan 2013, WISSARD project): about 130,000 cells per mL, similar to
  much of the deep ocean. Living on rocks and minerals, not sunlight. Nature, Aug 2014. [14]
- **Lake Mercer** (under 1,087 m of ice, SALSA project, drilled Dec 2018): 1,374 single-cell genomes; most are **new species,
  genetically isolated** from ocean and surface life, with flexible metabolisms (many can live with or without oxygen). Their
  ancestors may have been under the ice since glaciers spread ~30 million years ago (a suggestion, not proven). Nature
  Communications, 18 Aug 2025. [15]

## 3. How long DNA lasts, and what has actually been "revived"
*Summary: DNA breaks down steadily, even frozen. Usable DNA tops out around 1–2 million years. Living cells revived from the
cold are real, but the confirmed ages are in the tens of thousands of years (and one 100-million-year seafloor case).*

**DNA's clock**
- In bone, DNA has a **half-life of about 521 years** (moa bones, New Zealand, Allentoft et al. 2012). Even at -5 °C, every bond
  would be broken after ~6.8 million years; usable stretches top out around 1–2 million years. [7]
- Frozen ice cores: DNA fragments shrink over time; one Antarctic study estimated a half-life of ~1.1 million years for the
  DNA in its ice (Bidle et al. 2007). [9]
- Sediment beats bone: DNA stuck to clay and mineral grains lasts longer, which is why the record (~2 million years) comes from mud. [6][8]

**Things revived from the cold (the real list)**
| What | From where | Age | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bacteria (aerobic) | Seafloor mud, South Pacific | up to 101.5 million years | Revived and multiplied in the lab (Morono et al. 2020). Not ice; buried in mud. [17] |
| Bacteria | Antarctic Dry Valleys ice (Bidle 2007) | ice up to ~8 million years | Only weak growth from the oldest ice; DNA badly damaged; age and contamination questioned. [9] |
| Bacteria | Vostok refrozen lake ice | thousands to tens of thousands of years (age of lake water not pinned) | Grown in lab (Christner 2001). [12] |
| Pandoravirus yedoma + 12 other giant viruses | Siberian permafrost | 27,000–48,500 years | Infected amoebae in the lab (Alempic et al., Viruses, 2023). [11] |
| Pithovirus sibericum (giant virus) | Siberian permafrost | ~30,000 years | Infects only amoebae (PNAS 2014). [10] |
| Nematode worm *Panagrolaimus kolymaensis* | Siberian permafrost | ~46,000 years | Revived, reproduced 100+ generations (2023). Some scientists suspect modern contamination. [18] |
| Bdelloid rotifers (tiny animals) | Siberian permafrost | ~24,000 years | Revived, fed, reproduced (Current Biology 2021). [19] |

**Key point:** the "zombie virus" headlines are permafrost, not Antarctic ice, and the viruses were picked on purpose because they
only infect amoebae. [10][11]

## 4. Dead DNA vs living cells
*Summary: Dead DNA can't "come alive", but bacteria can swallow loose bits of DNA and use them. That is a real, named
process: transformation. Living revived microbes are a different case: they have to compete.*

**Dead DNA**
- Loose DNA in seawater breaks down within days. Stuck to clay or iron minerals in mud, it can last up to ~2 million years. [8]
- Some bacteria can **take up loose DNA from their surroundings and stitch it into their own genome.** Biologists literally call
  this **"natural transformation."** Moving genes sideways between living things (instead of parent to child) is called
  **horizontal gene transfer.** [8][GK]
- Lab work shows bacteria can take up DNA that is stuck to clay and iron minerals. The ocean-floor top 10 cm alone holds
  ~0.4 billion tonnes of loose DNA. A 2024 paper argues ancient DNA in sediments could fuel evolution of today's bacteria;
  that is **a hypothesis supported by lab experiments, not something seen happening in the wild with ancient DNA.** [8]
- In practice, most ancient DNA is short, broken pieces. A broken piece is much less likely to be useful to a living bacterium. [7][8]
- **No Antarctic or ocean case of a bacterium picking up a specific ancient gene from melting ice has been documented: not established.**

**Living cells**
- A revived microbe has to find food, survive viruses and predators, and beat the trillions of microbes already adapted to today.
  Most dormant microbes woken in the lab grow slowly. [9][17]
- Antarctic soils already carry very old natural antibiotic-resistance genes (177 found at Mackay Glacier sites), and 30,000-year-
  old permafrost DNA contained resistance genes too (D'Costa et al., Nature 2011). Lesson: "ancient" genes are often **already
  out there**; resistance is old and natural. [20][21]

## 5. "Would it change into a new species?" How evolution actually works
*Summary: The environment doesn't rewrite DNA. Random copying mistakes (mutations) happen, and the environment decides which
survive. Over many generations that can make something new. Microbes can also trade genes.*

- **Mutation:** every time a cell copies its DNA, rare mistakes happen. Radiation and chemicals add a few more. These changes are
  random; they are not "aimed" at the new environment. [GK]
- **Selection:** in a new environment, cells whose random changes happen to help them grow faster leave more offspring. Over many
  generations the population shifts. That is evolution. [GK]
- **Gene swapping:** bacteria add a shortcut: they can pick up whole working genes from neighbors (section 4). [8][GK]
- Microbes divide fast (hours to weeks), so change can show up in years, not millions of years. The 2007 Antarctic bacteria
  doubled only about every 70 days, so very slowly. [9][GK]

**What would likely happen to an ancient microbe dropped in today's ocean** (scientists' general expectation, not a measured result):
1. **Most likely: it loses.** Today's microbes are tuned to today. Dormant cells are often damaged. [9][17][GK]
2. **Some may adapt** if they find a niche, changing gradually through mutation and selection.
3. **Bits of their genes may get absorbed** into modern microbes through gene swapping.
A brand-new species appearing from this specifically: **not established**. It is possible in principle, but nobody has seen it.

**What was different back then**
| Thing | Then | Now |
|---|---|---|
| CO2 | 172–300 ppm over the last 800,000 years (ice-core record) [16]; ice-core work suggests below ~300 ppm for the last ~3 million years [22] | ~431–432 ppm monthly average in mid-2026, a record [22] |
| Oxygen | About the same; it fell only ~0.7% over 800,000 years (Stolper 2016) [23] | ~21% of air |
| Temperature | Allan Hills region cooled ~12 °C over the past 6 million years [1] | Warming now |
| UV | Before the 1980s there was no ozone hole | Antarctic ozone hole each spring since ~1980; 2025's was the 5th smallest since 1992; recovery to 1980 levels expected ~2066 [24] |
| Gravity | Essentially the same | Essentially the same [GK] |

**About gravity (say this gently):** Earth's gravity depends on Earth's mass and size, and neither has changed in any meaningful
way over millions of years. Life in the ice grew up under the same pull we feel today. What did change is the air (CO2), the
temperature, sea level and, recently, ozone/UV. [GK]

## 6. Is there a real disease risk from melting ice?
*Summary: Scientists say the risk is low but not zero, and they watch it. The best-documented real case came from a thawed
animal grave in Siberia, not from ancient Antarctic ice. Antarctica's actual current disease problem is bird flu brought in from outside.*

- **2016 Yamal anthrax (Siberia):** 2,350 reindeer died, a child died, dozens of people infected. The leading explanation is
  a heatwave thawing an old infected carcass, plus vaccination stopping in 2007. The carcass link is likely but not directly proven. [25]
- **2025 review (Mackelprang et al., mSystems):** the risk from permafrost viruses is "no greater" than from viruses in ordinary
  soil and water; microbe viruses jumping into humans has no precedent. [26]
- Earlier expert workshops (2019, and the US National Academies, 2020) called it "low probability, high consequence": low, not zero. [26]
- The scientists who revived the giant viruses argue the risk should be taken seriously and monitored. [11]
- **Antarctica specifically:** most known pathogens need a host (people, animals). Ancient ice in the deep interior has had almost
  no hosts around for a very long time. **A documented pathogen coming out of Antarctic ice: none found.** [GK; inference]
- **The real Antarctic disease story today is H5N1 bird flu, which arrived from outside.** First mainland cases in skuas Feb 2024,
  then Adélie penguins (Mar 2024); it killed huge numbers of elephant seal pups in Patagonia in 2023. [27]
  That is why biosecurity is strict (section 8).

## 7. Melting today: is the old DNA actually coming out?
*Summary: Antarctica loses roughly 100–135 billion tonnes of ice a year, mostly from the West and the edges. The oldest ice is
deep in the interior, near bedrock, and is not melting.*

- **NASA (GRACE satellites, 2002–2025): about 135 billion tonnes of ice lost per year** on average. East Antarctica gained a little
  from more snow; West Antarctica lost much more. [28] An independent reanalysis gets ~101 ± 89 billion tonnes/yr. [28]
- **IMBIE 2023** (50 satellite estimates combined, 1992–2020): West Antarctica -82 Gt/yr, Peninsula -13 Gt/yr, East Antarctica
  roughly balanced. Greenland + Antarctica together raised sea level 21 mm since 1992. [29]
- Most Antarctic loss is from **warm ocean water melting floating ice shelves from underneath** (55% of shelf loss in a 2013 study)
  and icebergs breaking off. [30]
- **Sea ice (frozen ocean, not ice sheet):** record lows in 2023; the Feb 2026 minimum was 2.58 million km² (16th lowest in 48 years);
  2026 winter peak was 3rd smallest. Sea ice is frozen seawater, only a year or a few years old, so it holds no ancient DNA. [31]
- **Is ancient DNA being released?** The oldest ice (Beyond EPICA, Dome C) sits ~2.8 km down, at bedrock, in the coldest, most
  stable interior. Melting happens at the edges, where the ice is much younger, because ice slowly flows outward. [3][28][30][GK]
  Some old ice does reach the surface in blue-ice areas, but there it mostly evaporates in dry cold wind rather than melting. [1][GK]
  **How much ancient DNA/microbes reach the ocean from melt today: not established (no measurement found).** Meltwater under
  the ice does carry modern subglacial microbes, like those in Whillans and Mercer. [14][15]

## 8. Why you can't just go, and how people actually do
*Summary: Antarctica belongs to no one. A treaty puts it aside for peace and science, and a 1991 add-on protects its environment.
You can go, but only through a permitted operator, with strict cleaning rules, and it costs a lot.*

- **Antarctic Treaty, signed 1959, in force 1961.** 12 countries at first; **58 parties as of April 2026, 29 of which vote**
  (Consultative Parties). Antarctica is for peace and science; no new territory claims; no military activity. [32]
- **Madrid Protocol (1991, in force 1998):** bans mining, and requires an **environmental impact assessment** for every activity,
  including tourism, filed in advance with the operator's home country. In the US, the EPA handles it (40 CFR Part 8). [33]
- **Landing limits (Measure 15, 2009):** ships carrying more than 500 passengers can't land anyone; **max 100 people ashore at once**,
  1 guide per 20 people, one ship per site at a time. Formal legal status still waiting on ratification, but the same limits
  are in the IAATO industry rules. [34]
- **Biosecurity:** boots scrubbed and disinfected (usually with Virkon S) at the gangway before and after every landing; clothes and
  bags vacuumed for seeds; nothing brought ashore that could carry life. Accidental seeds and insects have reached Antarctica on
  visitors' clothes. [35]
- **Tourism numbers (IAATO):** 2023–24 peak: **122,072** visitors; 2024–25: **118,491**; 2025–26: ~**113,500** (down ~4%,
  partly due to the 2024 heavy-fuel-oil ban and tighter big-ship rules). The 2025–26 figure is from secondary reporting, not
  IAATO's final paper. [36]
- **Cost:** expedition cruises about **US $6,000–$50,000 per person**; most around $10,000–$20,000; flights to South America
  (usually Ushuaia, Argentina) extra. [37]
- **How regular people go:**
  1. **Cruise** with an IAATO member, from Ushuaia (2 days across the Drake Passage) or fly-cruise from Chile. [36][37]
  2. **Support jobs**: the US Antarctic Program hires cooks, cleaners, carpenters, mechanics, drivers, etc. through the Antarctic
     Support Contract (Leidos and subcontractors). Hiring mostly Mar–Aug for the next season; physical and dental exams required. [38]
     Other countries' programs (UK, Australia, NZ) hire too.
  3. **Science**: grad students, technicians, field assistants on funded projects. [38]

## Surprising true facts (episode-ready)
1. There's ice in Antarctica **6 million years old** with air bubbles from before humans existed. [1]
2. Microbes from **101.5-million-year-old** seafloor mud (dinosaur age) woke up and multiplied in a lab. [17]
3. A tiny worm frozen ~**46,000 years** in Siberia was revived and has had **100+ generations** of babies since. [18]
4. There are **lakes of liquid water under 1 km of ice**, full of life (~130,000 cells per mL in Lake Whillans), living on rock
   chemistry with no sunlight. [14]
5. Most of the microbes in Lake Mercer are **new species** found nowhere else, possibly cut off for millions of years. [15]
6. The oldest DNA ever read came from **mud, not bone**: 2 million years, Greenland, with mastodon DNA. [6]
7. Bacteria can **"eat" DNA** they find lying around and use the genes; biologists call it **transformation.** [8]
8. **Antibiotic resistance is ancient**: genes for it were found in 30,000-year-old permafrost. [21]
9. Tourists get their **clothes vacuumed** for seeds before going ashore. [35]
10. Even at -5 °C, DNA can't last forever: all of it is gone by ~**7 million years**. [7]
11. Ice cores show atmospheric oxygen has **barely changed (~0.7% drop) in 800,000 years**, while CO2 is now far above anything in that record. [16][23][22]

## Common misunderstandings
- **"Melting ice is releasing million-year-old life into the ocean."** The oldest ice is deep and stable; melting happens at the younger edges. [3][30]
- **"Scientists revived a zombie virus that could infect us."** The revived viruses infect amoebae and came from Siberian permafrost. [10][11]
- **"Ancient DNA could come alive."** Free DNA is not alive. It can only matter if a living bacterium takes it up. [8]
- **"A new environment rewrites an organism's DNA."** It doesn't. Random mutations happen; the environment picks which ones survive. [GK]
- **"Gravity was different back then."** It wasn't, in any meaningful way. Air, temperature and UV changed. [GK]
- **"Antarctica is a secret closed place."** ~113,000–122,000 people visit each year; you need a permitted operator and money, not special clearance. [36]
- **"Sea ice records show the ice sheet is melting fast."** Sea ice is frozen seawater, different from the land ice sheet. [31]
- **"The 8-million-year-old bacteria were revived."** Only weak growth, damaged DNA, and the age is still questioned. [9]
- **"The 1-million-year seafloor DNA was 2023."** It was published October 2022. [5]
