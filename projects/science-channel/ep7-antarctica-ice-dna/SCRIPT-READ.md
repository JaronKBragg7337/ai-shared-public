# Science ep7, Part 1: Antarctica: what's frozen in the ice

Read-through for listening. About 1154 words, roughly 7 minutes 10 seconds at the ep6 voice pace. Revised after the Codex fact check (FACTCHECK-codex.md, 2026-11-02).

## 01. The oldest ice

This is Antarctica, and its ice is a freezer for time. In a place called the Allan Hills, scientists found ice six million years old: the oldest ice ever directly dated, more than twice the old record. Inside are tiny bubbles of air, sealed in long before there were any humans to breathe it. The team dated it by measuring argon gas in those bubbles, and announced it in October twenty twenty-five.

## 02. Layers like tree rings

How does ice get that old? In the coldest places, snow builds up year after year. Each new layer lands on top, and the weight squeezes the old snow into ice, trapping pockets of air. Layer on layer, like the rings of a tree. In January twenty twenty-five, a European team called Beyond EPICA drilled about two point eight kilometers to bedrock and pulled up one unbroken record at least one point two million years long. The six-million-year ice is scattered old chunks, pushed up where the wind strips the snow away.

## 03. DNA in the mud

The ice is not the only freezer. In twenty nineteen, scientists drilled mud from as deep as one hundred seventy-eight meters under the floor of the Scotia Sea. Inside was DNA about one million years old. Some of it came from diatoms: tiny algae with shells of glass, at the bottom of the ocean food chain. Their DNA was younger, reaching back about five hundred forty thousand years. Cold, little oxygen and no sunlight helped keep it. The study came out in twenty twenty-two: the oldest confirmed DNA ever found in the sea.

## 04. How long DNA lasts

The oldest DNA from anywhere also came from mud: about two million years old, from northern Greenland, showing a lost forest with mastodons, reindeer, hares and geese. DNA can last longer when it sticks to tiny grains of clay and minerals. In bone, one study estimated that DNA in bone kept at minus five degrees would break apart completely over roughly seven million years. So most ancient DNA is short, broken pieces. And DNA by itself is not alive.

## 05. Transformation

But here is a twist from real biology. Some bacteria can pick up loose DNA from around them and stitch it into their own genes. Biologists call it natural transformation: genes moving sideways between living things, instead of parent to child. In the lab, they can even take up DNA stuck to clay. Could ancient DNA give genes to today's bacteria this way? Lab work makes it a real idea, but nobody has seen it happen in the wild.

## 06. Would it become something new?

Would old life turn into something new? When cells copy themselves, mistakes can slip in, and radiation or chemicals can cause changes too. Those changes are not aimed at what the cell needs. But over many generations, the cells whose changes happen to help leave more offspring. A microbe waking from the deep past would face trillions of rivals already tuned to today, and scientists expect it would often struggle. Gravity would feel almost the same. The air and the temperature have changed.

## 07. What has come back to life

Has anything really come back to life? A few things. Bacteria from seafloor mud more than one hundred million years old woke up and multiplied in a lab. A tiny worm frozen about forty-six thousand years in Siberia was revived and has had more than one hundred generations since, though some scientists suspect modern contamination. And a two thousand seven claim of eight-million-year-old bacteria from Antarctic ice? Only weak growth, damaged DNA, and the age is still questioned.

## 08. The zombie viruses

You may have heard about zombie viruses. They are real, but they came from Siberia, from frozen ground called permafrost, not from Antarctic ice. Scientists revived giant viruses up to about forty-eight thousand years old, and they picked ones that only infect amoebae, tiny single-celled creatures. Not people.

## 09. Lakes under the ice

Now go under the ice. Antarctica hides hundreds of lakes of liquid water, kept from freezing by the pressure above and the Earth's heat below. In twenty thirteen, a team drilled cleanly into Lake Whillans, under about eight hundred meters of ice, and found about one hundred thirty thousand cells in every milliliter, like much of the deep ocean. No sunlight. They live on the chemistry of rocks.

## 10. Lake Mercer: new species

Under more than a kilometer of ice lies Lake Mercer. Scientists read the genes of one thousand three hundred seventy-four single cells from it. Most appear to be new species, genetically different from known ocean and surface microbes, and their genes suggest many can live with oxygen or without it. Their ancestors may have been sealed in since glaciers spread about thirty million years ago. That part is a suggestion, not proven.

## 11. Is the melting dangerous?

So is the melting dangerous? NASA satellites show Antarctica lost an average of about one hundred thirty-five billion tonnes of ice a year from two thousand two to twenty twenty-five, mostly in the west and at the edges, where warm ocean water melts floating ice from underneath. Some of the very old ice lies deep inland, and at Allan Hills some is pushed up near the surface. Ice flows outward, and much of what is lost at the edges is younger. How much ancient DNA reaches the sea from today's melt is still an open question.

## 12. Disease risk, and the real problem today

And disease? Some scientists see the risk from thawing ancient material as low, but not zero, and it is watched. A twenty twenty-five review found viruses from thawing permafrost no riskier than viruses in ordinary soil and water. No disease-causing germ released from Antarctic ice has been documented. The real danger there today came from outside: H5N1 bird flu, found on the mainland in twenty twenty-four, in skuas and then in Adélie penguins.

## 13. Why you can't just go

That is one reason you can't just go. Several countries claim parts of Antarctica, but a treaty signed in nineteen fifty-nine sets those claims aside and keeps the continent for peace and science. Fifty-eight countries have joined. A later agreement bans mining and requires an environmental assessment for activities there, tourism included. Tour operators follow a limit of one hundred people ashore at a site at once. Tour operators normally scrub and disinfect boots at landings, and vacuum clothes for seeds.

## 14. How people actually go

Still, about one hundred eighteen thousand people visited in the twenty twenty-four to twenty-five season, most by ship. Expedition cruises cost about six thousand to fifty thousand dollars a person. There is another way in: get a job. The United States Antarctic Program hires cooks, cleaners, carpenters, mechanics and drivers, mostly from March to August for the next season, after physical and dental exams. A kitchen can be a door to the bottom of the world.

## 15. Close and hook

So here is what is frozen down there: six million years of ice, a million years of DNA in the mud, and lakes of new life sealed in the dark. Some very old ice lies deep inland, some at Allan Hills sits near the surface, and scientists keep watching the edges. But the ice doesn't only keep life. It keeps the sky. Heartbeat Observatory.
