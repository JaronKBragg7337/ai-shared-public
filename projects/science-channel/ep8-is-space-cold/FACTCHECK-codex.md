- **n01 — “Space is cold”: UNSURE.** Empty space has little matter to give it a single temperature, and sunlight can make objects hot. Fix: “Deep space has a very cold background, but objects in sunlight can get hot.” [NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/cobe/science/)
- **n01 — Big Bang glow is about 2.7 degrees above absolute zero: OK.** NASA measured the cosmic microwave background at about 2.725 kelvin. [NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/cobe/science/)
- **n01 — Space is bad at taking heat away: OK.** A vacuum removes the usual routes through surrounding air, though cooling still occurs by radiation and other methods. [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/smallsat-institute/sst-soa/thermal-control/)
- **n02 — Heat moves by contact, moving fluid and radiation: OK.** These are conduction, convection and thermal radiation. [NASA](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230001953/downloads/ENAE%20691%20Spring23%20Thermal%20Cottingham.pdf)
- **n02 — In space, “only the glow is left”: WRONG.** Radiation crosses a vacuum, but material can also be vented to carry energy away, as the spacesuit example later shows. Fix: “Across an empty vacuum, heat travels by radiation; some systems also carry heat away by releasing material.” [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/iss-research/spacewalk-research-and-technology/)
- **n02 — A thermos uses a vacuum to keep coffee hot: OK.** The vacuum reduces heat transfer by conduction and convection. [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/smallsat-institute/sst-soa/thermal-control/)
- **n03 — Radiation grows with area and absolute temperature to the fourth power: OK.** That is the Stefan–Boltzmann relationship for a surface with fixed emissivity. [NASA](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230001953/downloads/ENAE%20691%20Spring23%20Thermal%20Cottingham.pdf)
- **n03 — Doubling temperature means “sixteen times the heat”: UNSURE.** The sixteenfold rule applies to radiation emitted, not necessarily to net cooling after absorbed heat is counted. Fix: “Double that temperature and it emits sixteen times as much radiation.” [NASA](https://tfaws.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/TFAWS2015-SC-ISS-Payload-Thermal-Design.pdf)
- **n03 — A perfect dark surface at room temperature gives off about 460 watts per square meter: OK.** At 300 kelvin, the calculation is about 459 watts per square meter emitted, before subtracting incoming radiation. [NASA](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230001953/downloads/ENAE%20691%20Spring23%20Thermal%20Cottingham.pdf)
- **n03 — Things in space need big surfaces turned away from the Sun: UNSURE.** Cooling design depends on the heat load; not every spacecraft needs a large separate radiator. Fix: “Spacecraft with a lot of heat often need large radiator surfaces shielded from sunlight.” [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/smallsat-institute/sst-soa/thermal-control/)
- **n04 — The ISS has six radiators: UNSURE.** Six are in its main heat-rejection system, but the station also has other radiators. Fix: “Its main cooling system has six large radiators.” [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/sites/default/files/atoms/files/np-2015-05-022-jsc-iss-guide-2015-update-111015-508c.pdf)
- **n04 — Each main radiator is about 23 meters long, roughly a tennis-court length: OK.** Boeing gives 75 feet, or about 22.9 meters. [Boeing](https://boeing.mediaroom.com/2002-09-24-First-of-Twin-Boeing-built-Trusses-Ready-for-International-Space-Station)
- **n04 — Liquid ammonia carries heat to the radiators: OK.** The main system uses liquid anhydrous ammonia in its heat-transport loops. [SAE engineering paper](https://saemobilus.sae.org/papers/space-station-heat-rejection-subsystem-radiator-assembly-design-development-951651)
- **n04 — The radiators were built for about 70 kilowatts: OK.** The original engineering paper gives 70 kilowatts as the design need, not a measurement of today’s load. [SAE engineering paper](https://saemobilus.sae.org/papers/space-station-heat-rejection-subsystem-radiator-assembly-design-development-951651)
- **n04 — Ammonia freezes near minus 78°C, and controllers steer radiators to prevent freezing: UNSURE.** The freezing point is supported, and the radiators rotate, but I could not independently verify the stated control purpose from the opened sources. Fix: “Ammonia freezes at about minus seventy-eight degrees Celsius, and the radiators rotate to help manage heat.” [SAE](https://saemobilus.sae.org/papers/international-space-station-design-freeze-radiators-972345), [Boeing](https://boeing.mediaroom.com/2002-10-28-Boeing-built-P1-Truss-to-Join-Twin-Aboard-International-Space-Station)
- **n05 — Body heat ranges from about 70 watts to over 700 watts: OK.** A NASA-associated paper gives a range of roughly 70–730 watts. [NASA paper](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20070013698/downloads/20070013698.pdf)
- **n05 — Heat in an airtight suit “has nowhere to go”: WRONG.** Heat can leave the suit, but cooling must be controlled to keep the astronaut comfortable. Fix: “The suit traps much of that heat, so the astronaut needs a cooling system.” [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/johnson/spacewalk-spacesuit-basics/)
- **n05 — Astronauts wear underwear threaded with cool-water tubes: OK.** The liquid-cooling garment circulates water to collect body heat. [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/johnson/spacewalk-spacesuit-basics/)
- **n05 — The backpack sublimator freezes water and releases vapor: OK.** It carries heat away, using a separate water supply from the circulating garment loop. [NASA paper](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20120003778/downloads/20120003778.pdf)
- **n05 — A spacewalk can use about 3.5 kilograms of water: OK.** A NASA-associated paper estimates about 3.6 kilograms lost per astronaut on a typical spacewalk. [NASA paper](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20120004284/downloads/20120004284.pdf)
- **n06 — AI computers use large amounts of electricity, nearly all ending up as heat: OK.** The equipment’s electrical power becomes waste heat that must be managed. [Forethought](https://www.forethought.org/research/will-we-really-put-data-centers-in-space)
- **n06 — Solar panels in the right orbit see the Sun almost all the time: OK.** A suitable dawn-dusk orbit can provide near-continuous sunlight. [Google Research](https://research.google/blog/exploring-a-space-based-scalable-ai-infrastructure-system-design/)
- **n06 — Google says orbital panels can be up to eight times more productive: OK.** Google makes that estimate for the right orbit; it is an upper-end claim, not a guarantee. [Google Research](https://research.google/blog/exploring-a-space-based-scalable-ai-infrastructure-system-design/)
- **n06 — Starcloud flew an Nvidia chip in 2025 and trained a small model in orbit: UNSURE.** Starcloud reports both events, but I found no independent verification of the training run. Fix: “Starcloud flew an Nvidia chip in 2025 and says it trained a small AI model in orbit.” [Starcloud](https://www.starcloud.com/starcloud-1)
- **n06 — SpaceX requested up to one million data-center satellites: OK.** The FCC confirms the 2026 application; the request is not an approval. [FCC](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-26-113A1.pdf)
- **n06 — Blue Origin filed for more than 50,000 satellites: OK.** Its application requested up to 51,600. [FCC](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-420864A1.pdf)
- **n07 — WEF estimated 1,600 square meters of radiator per megawatt: UNSURE.** The number depends on a particular two-sided radiator at about 20°C, so it should not sound universal. Fix: “One analysis estimated about sixteen hundred square meters per megawatt for a radiator around twenty degrees Celsius.” [World Economic Forum](https://www.weforum.org/stories/artificial-intelligence/space-data-centres-cooling/)
- **n07 — IEEE estimated 1.4 square meters for one AI chip: UNSURE.** This is a model for a 700-watt H100 at 60°C, not a fixed requirement for every top chip. Fix: “IEEE Spectrum estimated about one point four square meters for one seven-hundred-watt chip at sixty degrees Celsius.” [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/orbital-data-centers-heat)
- **n07 — Radiators have weight and must withstand sunlight and debris: OK.** Those are real design and durability concerns, although repair options depend on the mission. [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/smallsat-institute/sst-soa/thermal-control/)
- **n07 — A startup CEO said launch economics do not yet work: OK.** Orbital CEO Euwyn Poon said so in an April 2026 interview. [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/on-prem/2026/04/15/orbital-datacenter-startup-admits-launch-economics-dont-fly/5219697)
- **n08 — One study put radiators at 14% of system weight and found launch cost more consequential: OK.** Those figures describe Forethought’s conservative model, not a measured satellite. [Forethought](https://www.forethought.org/research/will-we-really-put-data-centers-in-space)
- **n08 — Google plans two test satellites in early 2027: UNSURE.** Google’s original announcement said early 2027, but its more recent update says 2027 without reaffirming “early”; Google also launched a separate prototype in October 2026. Fix: “Google has already launched one prototype and plans two more test satellites in 2027.” [Google prototype update](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/google-research/project-suncatcher-prototype/), [Google project update](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/google-research/google-project-suncatcher-facts/)
- **n08 — Whether space computing will beat ground computing is not established: OK.** Published estimates rely on uncertain launch costs, hardware and operating assumptions. [Forethought](https://www.forethought.org/research/will-we-really-put-data-centers-in-space)
- **n09 — “A vacuum is a nearly perfect blanket” and heat escapes only as a slow glow: WRONG.** Vacuum suppresses conduction and convection across empty space, but does not block radiation; vented material can also carry energy away. Fix: “A vacuum blocks the usual flow of heat through air. Spacecraft mainly cool by glowing, while some systems also release material.” [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/iss-research/spacewalk-research-and-technology/)
- **n09 — Radiators and water-cooled underwear address those cooling problems: OK.** Both are supported by the station and spacesuit sources above. [SAE](https://saemobilus.sae.org/papers/space-station-heat-rejection-subsystem-radiator-assembly-design-development-951651), [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/johnson/spacewalk-spacesuit-basics/)
- **n09 — Orbital data centers may be judged more by radiators than chips: UNSURE.** This is a prediction; the economic model also identifies launch cost as a major factor. Fix: “For orbital data centers, radiators and launch costs may matter as much as the chips.” [Forethought](https://www.forethought.org/research/will-we-really-put-data-centers-in-space)
