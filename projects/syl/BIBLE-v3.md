## 1. The game in one breath

It is 2080. People live on Earth, the Moon, Mars, Ceres and far out on Callisto. The bad solar storms of the 2070s burned out the
long-range relays, and every world turned inward. Mars kept the one neutral port anyone can reach.

You ride a transport to Mars with huge ships flying beside you. You land, you look at a board that describes every world and every
faction, and you pick one. You board a transport to that world. It crashes there. You survive, and you build your way back up to
space.

Two groups of people share your world, and they want different things. They both want you. If they work together, your world
builds bigger ships, a shipyard, a relay, a station, and reaches out early. If they compete, your world stays home and gets rich,
crowded, armed and flashy, and reaches out later from strength. Both are real ways to play. Either way you can fly anywhere from
day one, in a small ship, slowly, and when your ship lands on another world, those people are strangers. Trade with them, build
with them, or fight them. Every meeting is that choice again.

Seasons pass. Each one starts again through Mars. What you built at home stays. What you built out there does not. Only nature
forces anyone's hand.

The Solar System is the real one: real planets, real sizes, real distances, real day and night. The stuff on them is partly made up.

## 2. Four pillars

1. **Real where it counts.** Our Solar System, real bodies, real sizes and shapes, real gravity, real distances, real day and
   night. The ground is solid and can be dug in any direction. Earth has its real continents and oceans. **This is Game 1, semi
   sci-fi:** the resources, fuels and tech on worlds other than Earth are invented where real knowledge is thin, and alien-looking
   ships exist. Game 2, later, with a team and money, is the exact-real one. Builders: never compress a planet, never fake a
   distance, and say which things are fiction.
2. **Yours.** Your ship, your crew, your pad, your base. Every ship is walkable with real rooms. You own things with records and
   history. Things you own can be lost, stolen, repaired, sold, and carried from season to season if they are at home.
3. **Together, and fine alone.** One persistent shared world. Everyone sees the same holes, the same prices, the same wrecks.
   **NPCs fill every role until a human takes it:** pilots, traders, the gas station worker, governors, faction leaders, whole
   factions. The game is beatable solo and works the same with one player or a hundred thousand. Humans take a role by showing
   up, earning it, or being voted in.
4. **Earned.** Dig, haul, trade, build, fight. Nothing is given. Money has three honest numbers (what you settled, what is in the
   air, what you can spend). No pay-to-win, ever.

## 3. The setting: 2080, humanity's push outward

### 3.1 What happened (short version, for players)
- 2030s to 2050s: the Moon gets its first permanent bases. Mars gets a research colony that grows into a port.
- 2060s: the asteroid belt opens. Ceres has water ice. Water is fuel, air and farms. People move there. A research institute goes
  out to Jupiter and settles on Callisto.
- 2070s: a run of bad solar storms (the first COMMS FALL events) burns out the long-range relays. The big ships stop being built.
  Each world turns inward. Earth's weather turns worse. Meteors fall. People start to leave.
- 2080, now: Mars is the one neutral port anyone can reach, and strange ships have started showing up at the edge of sight.

### 3.2 The map at launch
All faction worlds ship together (Jaron: no half game).

| World | Who lives there | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Earth** | Homeguard and Skyward (proposed, section 4.5) | Real continents and oceans, no cities: players build where they want. Realistic resources. |
| **The Moon** | Fortis and Technos Prime | Real Moon, real craters. |
| **Mars** (with Phobos and Deimos) | Nobody. Neutral, NPC-run. | The one built world today. The hub everyone passes through. |
| **Ceres** | Ironclad and Greenhaven | The Belt's capital. Water, metal, farms. |
| **Callisto** | Mystara, plus one open seat | Jupiter fills the sky. The frontier that is already settled. |
| **The Belt** | Outposts, the pirate rock, Wanderhome way-stations | Vesta, Psyche, small rocks, wrecks, buoys. |
| **Stations** | Wanderhome (the Long Walk, Mars orbit) and Corsair's Refuge (a hidden rock) | Section 4.7 |
| **Later** | Ganymede, Europa, Titan, Saturn's rings, Venus | No home faction. Opened by what players build. |

Every planet spins at its real rate. Every moon orbits. Mars has a 24.6 hour day; Ceres turns in 9 hours; Earth in 24. Builders:
this is package F2 and it is not optional.

### 3.3 Reasons to go to space
- **The alien-looking ships.** Now and then a ship nobody recognises comes close to a port, a convoy or a lone pilot, holds for a
  moment, and speeds away faster than anything we have. Never explained in Game 1. The scanner logs it. People talk. Chasing one is
  a reason to build a faster ship.
- **Home-world disasters.** Solar storms, meteor falls, dust storms on Mars, hurricanes on Earth. A bad enough one can wreck a
  district and force people off a world for a while. Nature is the only thing in the game that forces anyone to move.
- **Money.** Prices differ by world on purpose (section 11).
- **Everyone else is out there.** Section 4.

## 4. Worlds and the two factions on each (the heart of the game)

Jaron's idea (10/3 9:50 AM): each settled world has **two factions** who share it. They compete, or they work together.

Jaron's addition (10/3 9:53 AM): **competing is not a punishment. It is the other strategy.** A world whose pair cooperates expands
early. A world whose pair competes stays home and gets very wealthy, very defensive, very full: big flashy cities, lots of players,
strong on their own ground. They can always change their mind, make peace, and expand later from strength. Neither path is wrong.

Jaron's rule (10/3 10:46 AM): **cooperation unlocks bigger things, never access.** Anyone can fly to any world from day one in a
small ship. What cooperation buys is bigger ships, stations, cheaper fuel, relays that work in a storm, and the right to build out
there in strength.

### 4.1 Where everyone lives

| World | Faction pair | Neutral place on it | Good at | Lacks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Earth** | **Homeguard** (stay, rebuild, fortify) and **Skyward** (launch, leave, expand) | Any of the big launch sites (proposed: one per continent, NPC-run) | People, food, water, everything in bulk, real resources | Easy launches (deep gravity well), peace with its own weather |
| **The Moon** | **Fortis** (the military bloc) and **Technos Prime** (the tech people) | Tranquility Civil Hub | Power, launch mass, security, electronics, comms, computing | Water (only polar ice), food, metal in bulk |
| **Mars** | None. Neutral, unownable, NPC staff. | Marineris Port (built today) | A port anyone can reach, trade, exchange, hiring, the Survey Office, Phobos and Deimos | A home faction. Nobody owns anything on Mars. |
| **Ceres** | **Ironclad** (the miners) and **Greenhaven** (the farmers and medics) | Kerwan Landing | Water ice, fuel, metal, farms, hulls, people who fix things | Electronics, security, long-range comms |
| **Callisto** | **Mystara** (the researchers) and **one open seat** | Valhalla Basin camp | Maps and data of the Jupiter system, safe ground, the way to Europa, strange finds | Industry. They cannot build much. |
| **Stations** | **Wanderhome** (the nomad network) and **Corsair's Refuge** (the pirates) | n/a | Section 4.7 | n/a |

Nine named factions plus an open seat. Every one of them runs itself with NPCs: an NPC governor, NPC traders, NPC haulers, NPC
patrols. A human can take any of those jobs. If no human ever shows up on Ceres, Ceres still mines, farms, feuds and makes peace on
its own, slowly.

### 4.2 The Moon: Fortis and Technos Prime

**Fortis** holds the south pole at Shackleton crater: the ice, the power towers on the rim that see the sun almost always, and the
mass driver that throws cargo into space without fuel. They are the military bloc: the people who kept the Moon alive when the storms
cut it off. Walls, gates, searchlights, red lights, numbered everything. Their look is the Unreal Fortis art Jaron already saw.
Strong: armor, fleet tools, defence, launch mass. Weak: loud, visible, slow to change.

**Technos Prime** lives on the far side, in and around Daedalus crater, where the Moon's body blocks Earth's radio noise. They run the
big dishes, the relay at the far-side halo orbit, the chip fabs and the software. Glass, white panels, blue light. Strong: scanning,
comms, hacking, electronics, diplomacy. Weak: thin hulls, no heavy industry, few people who can hold a gun.

**What each needs from the other.** Fortis needs navigation computers, long-range comms, ship electronics. Technos needs power, the
mass driver, armored hulls, protection.

**Cooperation unlocks** (in order): shared use of the mass driver (cheap launches for everyone), the far-side relay (comms through a
storm), the **Lunar shipyard** (big hulls), and a joint station at the Earth-Moon L2 point.

**The competing Moon** (the home path): the mass driver stays Fortis-only and the relay stays Technos-only, so each side sells the
other what it cannot make and both get rich doing it. Shackleton grows walls, gun towers and a huge garrison; Daedalus grows glass
towers, neon and the best electronics in the system. Checkpoints at the rille, sabotage jobs, two markets with fat margins. Home
advantages: the strongest ground defences anywhere, cheap power and launches for Moon ships near the Moon, the best sensors. Cost:
no Lunar shipyard, so Moon players fly small ships until the pair makes peace. When they do, they build the shipyard with money an
early-expanding world never had.

### 4.3 Ceres: Ironclad and Greenhaven

**Ironclad** mines Occator crater: the bright salt flats, the brine, the metal under them. Pit heads, smelters, gantries, amber work
lights, hard hats. They build hulls. Strong: mining, metal, shipyards, drills, haulers. Weak: strikes, supply shocks, nobody to grow
their food.

**Greenhaven** farms the Kerwan basin under long glass domes, melts the ice, keeps the air clean, runs the clinics. Wood and grown
material, green light, gardens. Strong: water, fuel from water, food, medicine, bio-repair, morale. Weak: they cannot build a hull
and they cannot defend one.

**What each needs from the other.** Ironclad needs food, water, air, doctors and fuel. Greenhaven needs metal, hulls, reactors,
drills and protection.

**Cooperation unlocks:** the **Ceres water-fuel refinery** (cheaper fuel for the whole Belt, later for everyone), the Belt shipyard
(mining rigs, ore barges, big haulers), farm ships, deep-Belt outposts (Vesta, Psyche).

**The competing Ceres** (the home path): Ironclad sells metal dear and Greenhaven sells water dear, and both pile it up. Occator
becomes a smelter city with its own militia and the biggest hull stockpile in the Belt; Kerwan becomes a dome city of gardens,
clinics and showy glasshouses. Strikes and water rationing are the weapons. Home advantages: the deepest fuel and metal reserves
anywhere, hulls on hand the day the pair makes peace, the healthiest crews. Cost: no refinery and no Belt shipyard. The pirates get
rich selling to both sides in the meantime.

### 4.4 Callisto: Mystara and the open seat

**Mystara** is the research institute that went out to Jupiter in the 2060s and went quiet in the storms. Their base is in the
Valhalla basin on Callisto, the one big Jupiter moon outside the worst radiation. They study Europa's ocean and the odd signals from
it, and they have the best records of the alien-looking ships (all game fiction, kept vague on purpose). Standing instruments,
sealed doors, violet and gold glyph light. Strong: maps, data, the only safe routes in the Jupiter system, strange tech. Weak: they
cannot build or fight, and they are few.

**The open seat:** the second faction on Callisto is whichever faction's players land there and raise a building first. From then on
Callisto has a pair too and the same rules apply. Until then an NPC prospectors' camp holds the seat loosely.

**Cooperation unlocks:** Europa access, Jupiter fuel skimming, the Saturn route.
**Competing at Callisto:** Mystara's data stays locked and the sealed sites stay sealed; the newcomers mine and fortify Callisto on
their own and get rich on Jupiter's moons without the maps. Expeditions get lost. The Saturn route waits for peace.

### 4.5 Earth: Homeguard and Skyward (proposed names)

Earth is playable: real continents and oceans from free data (credited), no real cities. Players build where they want. Earth's
resources are the real ones: iron, oil, water, farmland, forests, and the deep gravity well that makes every launch cost.

**Homeguard** wants to rebuild Earth and stay. They run the sea walls, the farms, the big fortified cities players will raise, the
militias. They are the home path made into a faction: rich, defensive, flashy. Strong: people, food, water, industry in bulk, the
best ground game anywhere. Weak: launches are expensive and their ships are few.

**Skyward** wants everyone off. They run the launch sites, the orbital tethers if players build them, the training. They sell Earth's
bulk to the other worlds and buy their way into space. Strong: launch sites, orbital know-how, trade in bulk goods, allies out
there. Weak: thin on the ground, dependent on Homeguard for everything they ship.

**What each needs from the other.** Homeguard needs what only space can give: rare metals, chips from the Moon, medicine from Ceres,
and warning of the next storm from the Technos relay. Skyward needs Homeguard's food, water, steel and people to fill its ships.

**Cooperation unlocks:** the first orbital tether (launches cheap enough for bulk trade), Earth's orbital yard, the Earth-Moon run
as a timetable, Earth as the breadbasket of the system.

**The competing Earth:** Homeguard builds the biggest, richest cities in the game and sells launches dear; Skyward runs a few
precious launch sites like a cartel. Home advantages: nobody out-produces Earth on the ground, and nobody takes an Earth city.
Cost: everything leaving Earth costs a fortune, so Earth players arrive out there late and rich.

### 4.6 Two paths per world (mechanics, not menus)

Every settled world has one **balance** between its two factions, from full competition to full cooperation, and one **home
strength** meter: wealth, industry, defences, population. The server holds both. Players move them. Neither is a slider on a
screen; both show in the place: who is at the checkpoint, whether the gate between districts is open, the notice board, the prices,
how tall the towers are.

Cooperation spends home strength on reach. Competition keeps it at home and grows it.

| | Cooperate early (the expanders) | Compete at home (the builders) |
|---|---|---|
| What the world looks like | Busy ports, joint yards, ships leaving, a thin treasury | Walls, towers, neon, crowds, two rich markets, a fat treasury |
| What players get | Bigger ships sooner, stations out there, cheaper routes, relays in a storm, allies, a head start on territory | Money: fat margins, cheap home prices for your own side, the best ground defences, home-only ship upgrades, the biggest cities |
| Home-ground advantages | Modest | Strong: faster repairs, cheaper fuel and power at home, more guns on the walls, raiders pushed out of the sky, your own side's dealers give you the best prices |
| Away from home | Strong: contacts, routes, big hulls | Thin at first: small ships, no allies, strangers to everyone (but anyone can still go) |
| Across seasons (section 5) | Off-world stations are lost each season; reputation and vote weight carry | Home holdings carry whole; the builders compound season over season |
| The pivot | Can turn to competition later and lose reach slowly | Can make peace at any time. Peace spends the treasury on the unlocks fast: a rich world builds the shipyard in days, not weeks |
| Late game | First out there, thin at home, spread out | Late out there, but arrives with full holds, hulls in stock and a garrison; can buy, build or fight its way to the top |

What it cannot do is both at once: reach and hoard pull on the same money.

**What moves the balance:**
- **Jobs.** Each faction gives jobs. Some help only that faction (they pay best, they are won at the other's expense, they push
  toward competition and raise home strength). Some help both (a water run from Greenhaven to the pits, a comms part from Technos to
  Fortis; they push toward cooperation). The board says which kind in plain words. NPCs take the jobs nobody takes, slowly.
- **Joint projects.** Big things (the shipyard, the relay, the refinery, the tether) are built from parts. Both factions deliver
  their half. Players haul the parts, drones assemble them (section 8). A project with one half delivered sits on the ground,
  visibly unfinished. A rich competing world that pivots can buy its missing half outright: that is the catch-up edge.
- **Home projects.** The competing path has its own build list: walls, gun towers, a bigger market, a bank, a palace, a parade
  ground, a dome city. Real buildings, built the same way, and they raise home strength. A world that competes for a long time is a
  world full of things to look at.
- **Sabotage and theft.** Pirate jobs to blow a pipe or steal a part. They pay well, push toward competition and raise your Heat with
  the side you hurt.
- **Peace deals.** A pivot is a job chain, not a vote: an envoy ride, a hostage swap, a joint convoy, a treaty signed on the shared
  pad. Any player can start it. It is always available.
- **Votes.** Faction leaders and world governors are elected (section 6). A leader sets the faction's stance, which tilts which jobs
  the board offers. NPC leaders hold the seat until a human wins it.
- **Nobody playing.** With no players, a world's NPCs run it: the balance drifts slowly toward competition and home strength holds.
  An empty world turns inward.

**What the balance does:**
- **Unlocks** go live in steps as the balance climbs. Each unlock is a real building or ship class at that world's port. Home strength
  sets how fast.
- **Prices** follow both meters. A competing world is dear for outsiders and cheap for its own. A cooperating world is cheap and busy
  for everyone.
- **Raiders** follow the balance, but home strength pushes them back: a rich fortified world has pirates in the lanes around it, not
  in its sky.
- **Ship size** follows the unlocks. Not range: a small ship can go anywhere, slowly, on expensive fuel. What a competing Moon cannot
  do is send a big ship out there until it makes peace.

### 4.7 The station factions: Wanderhome and Corsair's Refuge

Both are factions like the others (hang out, buy supplies, fuel, join, take faction jobs). Only the home is different: one main station
plus a spread of small ones (Jaron, 10/3).

**Wanderhome** (the nomads). Main station: **the Long Walk**, a patchwork of docked hulls and modules parked in high Mars orbit, because
Mars is the crossroads. Small way-stations sit along every route: fuel, a bunk, a notice board. Teal, canvas, lanterns, mismatched
everything. They are the couriers and the gossips: a nomad trader on Ceres knows what the Moon is paying for water this week. They
trade with everyone and need everyone's goods. Strong: routes, barter, modular ships. Weak: no government, no army, no home ground.
Wanderhome's station network is off-world for everyone, so it is rebuilt every season; that is their story.

**Corsair's Refuge** (the pirates). Main station: a hollowed asteroid somewhere in the Belt that nobody official knows the address of.
Players find it by rumour, by capture, or by being invited. Small dens hide near every world (a back alley at Marineris, a dark
hangar on the far side of the Moon, a wreck off Ceres, a smugglers' cove on Earth). Scavenged plates, stolen transponders on the wall,
graffiti. They sell weapons to both sides of every feud and forged licenses to anyone (section 13). **Pirates grow where worlds
compete and shrink where worlds cooperate**, but a rich fortified home world pushes them out of its own sky and into the lanes around
it. Pirates are always at war with everyone.

### 4.8 First contact

Anyone can fly to any world from day one. The first time a player from one world lands at another world's port is still recorded
and announced on both worlds, with the date and the players' names, because inside the game it is history. After that, the two
worlds choose the way a pair chooses: by what players do.
- **Trade** opens the moment two ships can dock. Prices differ wildly at first. The first traders across get rich. On purpose.
- **Alliance** is built: an embassy at each other's port, a shared relay, a joint station. Two allied worlds (four factions) can build
  what no world can alone: the Jupiter station, the Saturn route.
- **Rivalry** is also built: blockades, raids on haulers, buying up fuel, planting a pirate den in the other's orbit. A faction's
  leader (NPC or human) can declare war when enough of its players back it (a petition on the board). War makes the other side's
  ships fair targets. Mars stays neutral in every war.
- **Early versus late.** An early-expanding world has contacts, routes, relays and tech. A late one has money, hulls, crews and a
  fortress to come home to. A late world can buy into a route, outbid for fuel, or outgun a thin convoy. An early world can shut a
  late world out of its relays and its friends. Both have a hand.
- **Inside a world after contact:** the pair can split over it. Fortis may want war with Ceres while Technos wants trade. That
  reopens the home competition. Cross-world politics and home politics feed each other.

### 4.9 Why this stays fun alone, with one friend, or with a crowd

**Solo.** Every faction has NPC people doing its work whether or not any player is online. A solo player who delivers a joint
project's missing half can swing a world. A solo player on a competing world has a different good life: the best-paid jobs are at
home, the port is safe, and the pivot is a chain one person can start. Hired crew work at 70 to 85% of a good hand, so a solo
captain is always a little short-handed and always able to manage. **The game can be finished alone:** the main arc (section 10)
has an ending a single player can reach in a season.

**Two friends.** Start on the same world in rival factions and meet at the shared port. Start on different worlds and be each
other's first contact. Fly one ship together. All on two phones, on day one.

**A hundred thousand.** Factions have jobs that need many ships at once: escort a convoy, hold a pad while drones assemble a
station, run a blockade. Elections mean something. Wars have fronts. The NPCs step back as humans step up, one role at a time.

## 5. Seasons

Planned, never a surprise. The length is a setting (proposed default: 90 days; open question 1). Everyone knows the date.

**At the start of each season everyone re-enters through Mars:** the transport, the landing, the board, the choice, the crash (section
10). The crash has a different cause each season (a solar storm, a meteor, a pirate hit, a failed part), so it feels new and still
ends in a wreck.

**What carries over:**
- Your home-world holdings: buildings, land, stalls, bases on your home world.
- Ships parked at places you own on your home world.
- Reputation, with every faction.
- **Vote weight** earned from last season's progress, so veterans keep their standing (a long-serving president does not start as
  a nobody). Jaron's own account votes too, like anyone's.
- Your character, your look, your name, your record (first contacts, labels, section 13).

**What is lost:**
- Off-world bases and stations. The Jupiter station is rebuilt every season by whoever gets there.
- Ships left anywhere but a place you own at home.
- Money in the air: unpaid jobs, unsold holds.

**What this does to the two paths:** the builders compound at home across seasons. The expanders start each season with
reputation, contacts and vote weight, and rebuild out there faster each time because they know how. Both keep getting better at
their own thing.

**Only nature forces change.** Bullying, wars, alliances, blockades, betrayals between players are allowed and never undone. Solar
storms, meteors and weather are the only hands that move things nobody chose. A season can become its own story: the season the
Moon went dark, the season Earth and Ceres built the tether.

**Settled money** carries. Credits in your account are yours. Local currencies are exchanged at Mars on the way in.

## 6. Roles, votes and NPC stand-ins

Every seat in the game has a name on it. If no human holds it, an NPC does, and does the job in a plain, predictable way.

- **World governor** (one per world), **faction leader** (one per faction), **station commander** (one per station), **port master**,
  **trade director**, **security chief**. These set stances, fees within hard caps (planet tax 0 to 15%, station fees 0 to 20%),
  patrol budgets, who may dock.
- **Elections** happen on a schedule inside the season. Anyone with reputation in that faction can stand. Vote weight comes from
  this season's progress plus what carried over. NPC voters exist and vote predictably by faction stance, so a human candidate must
  actually win people over.
- **Jobs** (pilot, trader, hauler, gas station worker, miner, medic, patrol) are taken by showing up and doing them. An NPC steps
  aside when a human takes the post and steps back when the human leaves.
- **Mars** is the exception: its staff and traders stay NPCs forever. Nobody owns anything on Mars. You can rent a stall there; you
  cannot own one.
- **Whole factions** can be NPC-run for a whole season. They still trade, feud, make peace and fight, slowly and by the book.

### 8.3 The shipyard

A real yard at every major port: gantry, bay, ships standing on display. A board (and a phone sheet) with a picture of each ship,

### 8.4 Owning ships

- You start with no ship. You earn the first one from the wreck (section 10). Then you get a pad at the nearest port. The port grows
  pads as players arrive. A refresh never makes a second ship.
- You can own many ships. Each keeps its own pad, account and crew. One is your flagship.
- Ships are bought, found and repaired (a wreck you tow home), captured (a disabled raider), or traded player to player.
- Every ship carries a **license** from the faction that registered it (section 13). No license: no legal docking, fuel or repairs
  at that faction's ports.

- Upgrades: the parts shop and the repair page (engine, guns, shield, hold, sensors).
- Paint: hull colour and markings, faction decals, a name. Cosmetic, cheap, personal.
- At season end, a ship parked at a place you own at home comes with you. Any other ship is gone.

### 8.5 Crew

No cap. Hire a pilot, captain, navigator, gunners, comms, engineer. Each costs a signing fee and a wage per sol. Unpaid crew leave at
the next port. People to hire wait in the crew hall and walk out to meet you. Talk to whoever is flying to give orders. The player
does not need to be the captain.

## 9. Building: the endgame

Players build outposts, bases, cities and stations piece by piece, with drones. This is the bridge to Jaron's long vision (a later
version where play builds real things in real space), so it should be designed like real construction: parts, mass, sequence, time.

- **Parts** come from the economy: hull sections from a shipyard, trusses from a smelter, panels, tanks, a reactor, a dock collar. A
  building is a list of parts with a mass each.
- **Hauling** is the job. A Drayman carries parts. A convoy carries more. Pirates know when a convoy flies.
- **Drones assemble.** Park over the site, open the hold, and construction drones take the parts out one at a time and weld them in
  order. You can watch. Other players can watch. The half-built thing stands there between sessions.
- **Big projects need both factions** of a world, and the biggest need two worlds.
- **Players own what they build:** fees within caps, access rules, patrol budget. A player station on a Belt route is a business.
  On your home world it is yours for good. Off-world it lasts the season.
- **On Earth** players build cities from nothing on real ground. Homeguard's sea walls and farms are built the same way.
- First targets, in order: a relay mast at an outpost, a fuel depot in Mars orbit, the Long Walk's next module, the Lunar shipyard,
  the Ceres refinery, Earth's tether, the Jupiter station.

## 10. The opening and the story

### 10.1 The opening (Jaron's design, 10/3, public)

1. You ride a transport to Mars. Huge ships fly beside you, close enough to read their names. You can walk the passenger deck and
   look out. This is the game showing you space and ships in the first minute.
2. The transport lands at Marineris Port the normal way. You walk into the arrivals hall.
3. A board describes every world and every faction: a picture, a few plain lines, and the live numbers (how many players, the
   balance, home strength, prices for the main goods, who governs). You pick a world and a faction, or you pick a world and no side
   yet. (Staying on Mars with no side is allowed; you walk out of the port and start there; Mars gives you no home holdings to
   carry, so most people go.)
4. You board a transport to your world. On the way down, it crashes. The cause changes each season: a solar storm, a meteor, a
   pirate hit, a failed part, a bad landing in a storm. You survive.
5. You climb out of the wreck a few kilometres from the nearest port. You dig, you carry, you salvage. A rover comes. Its driver is
   from one of your world's two factions, picked at random, and makes the first pitch on the ride in. The other side's recruiter is
   waiting at the port.
6. You build your way back up to space: a worn starter hull to repair and earn at the port, or the wreck's lifeboat, depending on the
   world. Your first jobs are the port's jobs. Then the factions' jobs.

The surprise details (what is in the wreck, who the driver is, what happens at the port) stay in the builders' file. Each world
and each season's crash cause has its own variation there.

### 10.2 The story

Missions and story come after there is a full world to put them in (DECISIONS 10/3 8:39 AM). This is the shape, so builders leave

- **Recruitment.** Both factions of your world want you. Taking a faction's job is joining, slowly; there is no sign-up form.

- **Per-faction chains.** Each faction's chain tells its own story and ends in one of its unlocks. Tower people and port workers are

- **The pirates and nomads** have chains that run across all worlds.
- **Seasons** become stories: the season's crash, the season's storm, who won the votes, who got to Jupiter.
- **The Necklace** is Jaron's own franchise and stays separate from this game unless he says otherwise.

## 11. Economy

### 11.1 Money
- **Credits** are the common unit. Every world has its own currency, exchanged at Mars and at every port. Mars uses **marks**: 4
  marks = 1 credit (in code today). Proposed: the Moon's **lunars**, Ceres's **shares**, Earth's **dollars** (because it is Earth).
  Exchange rates drift with the balance between worlds; a trader can make money on the exchange alone.
- Three numbers, always separate: settled money, money in the air (goods in your hold at today's price, jobs not yet paid), and
  what you can spend right now.
- The starting purse and the wage table live in the builders' notes with the opening. Ratio to hold: a full day of honest digging
  earns a bit more than one gunner's daily wage. Hiring is affordable and never free.

### 11.2 Goods
Four classes: basic (water, food, fuel, oxygen), industrial (metal, alloy, composites, chips, parts), strategic (rare elements,
reactor cores, experimental material, Mystara's finds, the sci-fi fuels), illicit (contraband, stolen goods, forbidden tech, forged
licenses). Earth's goods are the real ones. Other worlds carry invented ones where real knowledge is thin, named plainly as such in
the material record. Every dug lot keeps its real mass and volume; the books always balance.

### 11.3 Where money comes from and goes
- **In:** jobs (survey samples pay 300 credits each today), hauling, selling dug material by the tonne, salvage (150 for the Phobos
  module), bounties (150 to disable a raider, 25 per escort drone), trade margins between worlds, stall sales to other players and
  to NPC buyers while you are away, rent from things you own.
- **Out:** wages, signing fees, fuel (free at your pad today; priced later), repairs, ammunition, docking fees, stall rent (700 marks
  for 7 sols today), licenses, taxes (planet 0 to 15%, station fees 0 to 20%, hard caps), insurance later.
- **Shops:** NPC dealers at every port; rentable stalls (six at Marineris today) where renters set prices; the shipyard; the parts
  shop; the license office; the black market in the back alley (10 to 30% of a port's trade keeps it healthy).

### 11.4 Trade between worlds
Prices differ by world on purpose. Water is dear on the Moon, cheap on Ceres. Chips the other way. Earth sells bulk cheap but
launches dear. Fuel follows the Ceres refinery. The board at Mars shows every world's prices (Wanderhome sells the freshest numbers).

Not levels. Things.

1. **Day one:** the crash. Walk, dig, carry, salvage, ride to the port. Meet both sides.
2. **First days:** earn the starter ship. Fly low. Do port jobs. Hire one person. Take a side, or not yet.

## 13. Switching sides: the full design (Jaron asked for one)

Switching is allowed. It leaves a mark. It is never a dead end. The rules below are the same for everyone, including NPCs.

### 13.1 What a faction gives you (so it is clear what you lose)
- **Licenses.** Four kinds: ship registration, ground vehicle, digging machine, and trade (the right to sell at that faction's
  markets). Each is a card on your record, issued by one faction, shown on the ship's transponder and the vehicle's plate.
- **Quest items.** Keys, access codes, faction gear, a captain's commission, the parts a chain handed you. Marked "issued by" on the
  item.
- **Prices.** Your own side's dealers give you the home price. Everyone else pays the outsider price.
- **A home.** Your holdings on your home world.

### 13.2 How you switch
- Walk into the other faction's hall (on your own world, or anywhere its recruiter stands) and take its oath job: one real job for
  the new side, done in the open. That is the switch. No form.
- You can join the other faction on your world, a faction on another world, Wanderhome, or the Corsairs.
- **One switch per season**, after the first 7 days of the season (so nobody switches to dodge a crash). A second switch in the
  same season is only possible through the Corsairs (section 13.5).

### 13.3 What happens the moment you switch
- **Your old faction's licenses are revoked.** The transponder goes red at their ports. You cannot dock, fuel, repair or sell at
  the old side's places legally. Their patrols can stop you.
- **Quest-issued items from the old faction are removed** (keys, codes, commission, gear). Things you bought or dug stay yours.
  Ships you bought stay yours; their old registration is void and they need a new one.
- **The new faction issues a probation license** at once: dock, fuel, repair at the new side's ports at outsider prices for 14
  days or until you reach their first reputation step, whichever comes first. Then full licenses, home prices.
- **Reputation with the old side drops** to its floor. Reputation with everyone else is untouched. Your vote weight is kept, but it
  counts only in your new faction.
- **Your home-world holdings stay yours.** If you switched within your world, nothing moves. If you switched to another world's
  faction, your old holdings still carry across seasons but you cannot run them from the old side's ports; you reach them through
  the neutral port or the black market.

### 13.4 Labels (what other players can do)
- Anyone in your old faction can pin **"traitor"** on your record. Anyone you shot while they were friendly can pin **"pirate"**.
  A label shows on your name tag to that faction's players and at that faction's ports. A label is a fact, not a punishment: it says
  what you did and who says so.
- Labels fade: 30 days with no new offence, or one season, whichever is longer. A faction leader (human or NPC) can lift one early.
- Nobody can label you for anything but a real recorded act (a switch, a friendly-fire hit, a theft). The server checks before the
  pin lands.

### 13.5 The routes around a revoked license
- **Other-faction routes.** Your new faction's ports serve you. Mars always serves everyone. Wanderhome way-stations serve
  anyone who pays.
- **The black market** sells forged licenses: they work until a patrol scans you close (then Heat, a fine, the forged card burned).
  They cost real money and they are how pirates live.
- **The Corsairs** take anyone. Joining them revokes every other faction's licenses and pins "pirate" on you everywhere. Leaving them
  costs a job for the side you want back, and your record keeps the pirate line for a season.
- **Coming back.** You can rejoin an old faction after one full season. Twice a traitor is a label that never fades.

### 13.6 Why this is fair
- You never lose what you earned by hand: dug material, bought ships, home holdings, settled money, reputation elsewhere.
- You always have a port that serves you (your new side, Mars, Wanderhome).
- Everything that bites is a thing the other side gave you: their licenses, their items, their prices, their trust.
- The cost is visible before you switch: the hall shows exactly what will be revoked and removed.
- NPCs switch too, under the same rules, so defection is part of the world's own story and never only a player trick.

## 14. Multiplayer rules

- **One shared persistent world.** The server holds everything that matters: ships, pads, money, holes, damage, crew, stalls,
  prices, seats. A refresh puts you back exactly where you were. Away while flying: the ship keeps flying. Died while away: that is
  on you.
- **Own ship, own pad,** once earned. More as you earn. Pads are allocated and kept for the season.
- **Fly together.** Any player can board a ship whose owner allows it. Seats are real stations. Voice chat is proximity and crew
  channel.
- **Shared ground.** Holes, heaps, damage, prices, the raider fleet, the port's stock: the same for everyone.
- **Mars is neutral.** No fighting on Mars, in its 1,500 m airspace, or at its orbit ring. Leaving neutral space is announced.
- **PvP everywhere else.** Any player can be attacked by any other player outside Mars. Pirates are always fair game and always
  at war.
- **Friendly fire is penalised.** Hitting a ship or person of your own faction: reputation loss on the first hit, a license
  suspension (ship registration, 24 hours) on the third in a season, and the "pirate" label is available to the one you hit. Your own
  faction's patrols will stop you. The server checks every hit; nobody is penalised for a miss or for hitting a pirate in your colours.
- **Bullying, wars, alliances and betrayals between players are allowed** and are never undone by the game. Only nature forces
  change.
- **Station factions** behave like the others. Their stations have their own neutral docking rings.
- **Solo world.** An offline save exists for play without a connection. It never merges with the shared world.
- **Guests and accounts.** Play as a guest on the device; sign in to keep the same character on any device and across seasons.
- **Phones.** Every keyboard action has a touch button. Everything is checked at phone width before it ships.

## 17. Rules for builders (short)

- Real-looking people everywhere (the Loft pipeline). Never blocky.
- Every seat has an NPC in it until a human takes it. Nothing waits on a player to exist.

- Old builds give ideas, never art or assets. Free data sources get credit.
- "Done" means walked and looked at: screenshots from several angles, inside and out, phone width, validator green, nothing floating
  or half-sunk. Fable reviews the pictures.
