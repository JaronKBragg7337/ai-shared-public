## s04 (space)
*Deep space, Sun a bright star behind. The live truck glides up beside Pioneer 10: hexagonal bus, big white 2.74 m dish facing Earth, two RTG booms, long thin magnetometer boom. The truck's tiny roof dish and Pioneer's big dish both point the same way, home.*

**VEX:** There it is. Pioneer 10, launched March 2, 1972. Its twin, Pioneer 11, launched April 5, 1973.

**JOE:** Pioneer 10 was the first spacecraft to fly past Jupiter, in December 1973. Then it just kept going.

**VEX:** Look at that dish. 2.74 meters across. It points at Earth, just like ours.

**JOE:** Ours is smaller.

## s05 (space)
*Slow orbit around Pioneer. Labels pop on: six-sided equipment box, two booms with two RTGs each glowing faint orange (false-color heat view, labeled), magnetometer boom, the gold plaque on the struts. A tiny radio wave ripples from the dish toward a far blue dot.*

**VEX:** Tour time. The body is a six-sided box full of electronics. Those two long arms hold four power units called RTGs. Inside is plutonium, and it stays hot for decades.

**JOE:** Like a campfire that never needs more wood. Only about five percent of that heat becomes electricity.

**VEX:** At launch: about 2,500 watts of heat, and about 160 watts of electricity.

**JOE:** And the skinny third arm?

**VEX:** A magnetometer, for measuring magnetic fields. It sits far out so the spacecraft's own electronics don't confuse it.

**JOE:** And the radio signal it beams home goes out at just eight watts.

**VEX:** Eight watts. About as much as a small old-style night-light bulb. From billions of miles away.

## s06 (space)
*Close on the gold plaque: the man and woman, the spacecraft outline, the pulsar map, the line of planets with a tiny probe leaving the third one.*

**JOE:** There's also a gold plaque on board. Two people, our planets, and a map to find our Sun using pulsars. Carl Sagan and Frank Drake designed it, and Linda Salzman Sagan drew it.

**VEX:** A business card, in case anybody out there finds it.

## s07 (board)
*Drawn board: Sun, Earth, and Pioneer far out on a dotted path. A radio wave travels home. A predicted dot and the real dot separate slightly, the real one nudged sunward. Big number appears.*

**JOE:** Now the mystery. Navigators tracked Pioneer by its radio signal. The way the signal's pitch shifts tells you how fast the spacecraft is moving.

**VEX:** Starting around 1980, when Pioneer 10 was about twenty times farther from the Sun than Earth is, the numbers stopped matching. Both Pioneers were slowing down a tiny bit too much.

**JOE:** Like something was gently tugging them back, roughly toward the Sun.

**VEX:** John Anderson's team at JPL measured it. About eight point seven ten-billionths of a meter per second, every second.

## s08 (board)
*Drawn board: a couch with a sleeping cat labeled 'Earth gravity' next to a speck labeled 'Pioneer pull'. Then a road map: Chicago to Detroit. Then a globe with a ring drawn once around it.*

**JOE:** That is a very small number. Help us out, Vex.

**VEX:** It's about eleven billion times weaker than the gravity holding you on your couch.

**JOE:** So it does nothing.

**VEX:** It adds up. If that pull stays steady, after one year the spacecraft is about four hundred kilometers from where the math says it should be. About the drive from Chicago to Detroit.

**VEX:** After ten years it's moving about one kilometer per hour slower, and it's more than forty thousand kilometers off. That's farther than once around the Earth.

**JOE:** Navigators notice that.

## s11 (board)
*Drawn board: boxes of old magnetic tapes stacked under a staircase, a vintage tape machine, a laptop reading the tapes, a progress bar labeled '43 GB'.*

**JOE:** Is it the universe, or the spacecraft? To find out, you need to know how warm every part of Pioneer was, for decades.

**VEX:** That information was on old tapes. In 2004, JPL scientist Slava Turyshev started tracking down the records. Some were in boxes stored under a staircase.

**JOE:** Viktor Toth, a programmer in Canada, helped build software to read the telemetry: the spacecraft's own reports about itself.

**VEX:** Members of The Planetary Society helped pay for the rescue, and NASA later did too. More than forty-three gigabytes of data.

**JOE:** Tiny today. Huge in 1972.

## s12 (space)
*Pioneer in false-color heat view: RTGs glowing orange, the equipment box glowing warm. Heat rays leave the RTGs, hit the back of the big dish and bounce off away from the Sun. More arrows leave the back than the front; a small arrow on the spacecraft points sunward.*

**VEX:** Here is what they found. Warm things give off heat as infrared light, a kind of light you can't see. And light gives a tiny push when it leaves.

**JOE:** Like a flashlight that pushes you backward. Very, very gently.

**VEX:** Pioneer glows with waste heat, from the power units and from the electronics in the box. Some of that heat hits the back of the big dish and bounces off, away from the Sun. More heat leaves out the back than the front.

**JOE:** So the spacecraft nudges itself toward the Sun.

**VEX:** And to explain the whole pull, the lopsided part only has to equal about sixty-three watts beamed one way. Out of roughly two thousand watts on board.

**JOE:** Sixty-three watts. About one old light bulb's worth of heat, steering a spacecraft.

## s13 (board)
*Drawn board: three report cards from three teams, each with a heat map of Pioneer. A big bar: 80% heat model filled in, the last 20% shaded as 'inside the error bars'.*

**JOE:** Other teams modeled that heat too. A team in Germany in 2011 said heat likely explains it. A team in Portugal in 2012 found heat covers somewhere between about half and nearly all of it, at one point in the trip.

**VEX:** Then Turyshev, Toth and their team published in Physical Review Letters in 2012. Their heat model, built from the rescued data, came out to about eighty percent of the pull they measured from the radio data.

**JOE:** Eighty. Not a hundred.

**VEX:** That gap fits inside the error bars. The biggest unknown is how the coating on the power units aged in space. Their conclusion: no anomalous acceleration remains.

**JOE:** So: mostly solved. A little fuzzy at the edges. No new physics needed.

## s14 (truck)

**JOE:** We last heard from Pioneer 10 in January 2003. Pioneer 11 went quiet in 1995.

**VEX:** The heat helped solve the mystery. And as it faded, Pioneer 10's power dropped until its signal was too weak to hear. Pioneer 11 ran low on power too, and Earth drifted out of its antenna's view.

**JOE:** Turn us around, Vex. We have a correction to file.

## What the brief missed or got slightly off

1. **Heat split:** the 2012 PRL thermal model gives about 80% of the radio-data estimate, and the electronics (~45 points) matter more than the RTGs (~35 points). "Heat from the RTGs bouncing off the dish explains essentially all" overstates it; the gap is inside the error bars. Script says it that way.
2. **Dates:** the data rescue began in 2004 (Doppler set complete by 2006); 2011 and 2012 are the analysis papers.
3. **The famous 8.74 ± 1.33 is the 2002 PRD value**; the 1998 PRL gave about 8.5 × 10⁻¹⁰ m/s².
4. **Direction:** later papers could not pin down whether the pull points at the Sun, Earth or along the spin axis; script says "roughly toward the Sun".
5. **Pioneer 11 went quiet for two reasons** (low power and Earth leaving its antenna's view); month differs by source (Sept vs Nov 1995).
6. **Caveats the paper itself lists:** Pioneer 11 checked less deeply, the spin-down of both craft not explained, outgassing not addressed. Hence "mostly solved".
7. **Grok quote:** the script treats Grok's pitch as a paraphrase. If the board shows the post, use a real screenshot of the actual X exchange.
8. **Pronunciation:** Turyshev is respelled "too-ree-shev" and Toth "Toath" in `say`; worth a check against a recording of either name if one turns up.
9. **Visual accuracy for the 3D builder:** Pioneer's dish faces Earth; the RTGs sit on two booms (two per boom) about 3 m out; the magnetometer boom is longer (about 6.6 m) and thinner; the plaque is on the antenna support struts. Heat-glow shots should be labeled false color.
10. **Vertical short:** the brief asks for one; a natural cut is s08 (how small is it) + s12 (heat push) + s15 (Grok tag), 284 words, about 1 minute 55 seconds; drop the flashlight and light bulb lines for a tighter cut.
